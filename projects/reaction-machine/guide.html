Clocks & counting · Project 03

The Reaction
Machine

Ten lights chase each other round a circle, fast. One of them is gold. Hit the STOP button at exactly the right moment and the machine says you won — out loud. Miss, and it says that too. Nothing in it is programmed; a resistor and a capacitor decide how fast time runs.

Age 10–12, with an adult nearby for the first hour Time 2–2½ hours, in 5 stages Voltage 5 V — nothing dangerous Chips CD40106, CD4017, CD4013, CD4081
Section 1

What you're building

Play it here first. It's the same circuit, same chips, same speeds.

A ring of ten lights. A dot runs round it, about twelve steps a second on the easy setting. You get one press of STOP. If the dot is sitting on the gold light when you press, the green lamp comes on and the buzzer goes off. If it isn't, the red lamp tells you so, and you press RESET to go again.

The three speeds are the three real resistor choices from Stage 5 — the numbers on the buttons are what you will actually plug in.

The whole idea in one line. A capacitor filling and emptying through a resistor makes a steady beat. A counter turns each beat into "light the next one". A flip-flop remembers that you pressed the button. An AND gate checks whether those two facts — stopped and on the gold one — are true at the same moment.
Section 2

Three things you haven't built yet

The Gate Bench and the Logic Vault were made only of gates. This one isn't.

In the first two projects, every circuit answered instantly and forgot instantly. Change an input, the output changes; let go, and it's as if nothing happened. Those are called combinational circuits, and they can't count, can't wait, and can't remember. This machine needs all three.

A clock

Something that goes 1 0 1 0 on its own, forever, with nothing telling it to. One inverter, one resistor, one capacitor. This is where time comes from.

A counter

Something that keeps track of how many beats have gone by, and lights the matching lamp. One chip, ten outputs, and it does the whole job.

A memory

Something that stays changed after you let go of the button. One flip-flop — and it is genuinely the same thing your computer's memory is made of, just one bit of it.

Why this order. Clock, counter, memory: that's the shape of every computer ever built. A processor is a clock driving a counter that keeps track of which instruction it's on, plus a great deal of memory. You are about to build the skeleton of one and then play a game on it.
Section 3

How to make a beat out of nothing

The one idea in this project that needs a proper explanation.

Start with an ordinary inverter — you met six of them in the Gate Bench. Now do something that sounds silly: connect its output back to its own input, through a resistor. And hang a capacitor from that input to the − rail.

A capacitor is a tiny rechargeable bucket for electricity. Current flowing in fills it and its voltage rises; current flowing out empties it and its voltage falls. The resistor controls how fast — a big resistor is a narrow pipe, so filling takes longer.

Now follow it round. Say the capacitor is empty, so the input is at 0. An inverter flips that, so the output is high, and current trickles back through the resistor and starts filling the capacitor. The input voltage climbs. At some point it gets high enough to count as a 1 — and the inverter flips. The output goes low, and now current drains out of the capacitor through the same resistor. The input falls until it's low enough to count as a 0, the inverter flips again, and we're back where we started. It never settles. It can't.

Why the CD40106 and not the CD4069. An ordinary inverter is vague about where 0 stops and 1 starts, and near that boundary it can dither. The CD40106 is a Schmitt trigger: it flips up at 2.9 V and back down at 1.9 V, and those two different thresholds are exactly what makes the swing wide, clean and countable. Both numbers are from the datasheet, at 5 V.

Because those thresholds are known, the beat is predictable. Multiply the resistor by the capacitor and the frequency falls out:

ResistorCapacitorR × C Beats per secondFeels like
100 kΩ10 µF1 s≈ 1.2 A slow, countable tick — Stage 1 and all debugging
1 MΩ0.1 µF ("104")0.1 s≈ 12 Game speed, easy setting
2 × 1 MΩ side by side0.1 µF0.05 s ≈ 25Hard
4 × 1 MΩ side by side0.1 µF0.025 s ≈ 49Nearly a blur

The formula, if you want it, is f ≈ 1.23 ÷ (R × C). The 1.23 comes from those two thresholds; you can derive it once you've met logarithms.

Yours will not be exactly 12 Hz, and that is fine. A "104" capacitor is allowed to be 20% off, and the trigger thresholds vary from chip to chip. Anything between 8 and 16 is normal. Nothing in this machine cares about the exact number — you're building a game, not a watch.
Two resistors side by side make a smaller resistor. That surprises everybody. Two pipes side by side carry more water than one, so the current has an easier time, so the resistance is lower — two 1 MΩ in parallel behave like 500 kΩ, and four behave like 250 kΩ. That's why adding resistors speeds the game up.
Section 4

How the machine thinks

Four chips, each with exactly one job.

  1. Keep time — CD40106 One inverter with its resistor and capacitor, ticking away. Its output is the clock: high, low, high, low, about twelve times a second.
  2. Count the ticks — CD4017 This chip has ten outputs and it makes exactly one of them high at a time, stepping to the next one on every rising edge of the clock. Ten LEDs, one per output, and the dot walks round the ring by itself.
  3. Remember the button — CD4013 Pressing STOP sets a flip-flop, and it stays set after you let go. That signal is called FROZEN, and it goes to the CD4017's clock inhibit pin — a pin whose entire purpose is "stop counting". The dot stops dead.
  4. Judge it — CD4081 One AND gate asks is it FROZEN and sitting on the gold light? — that's WIN. A second AND gate asks is it FROZEN and not on the gold light? — that's MISS. The "not" comes from a spare inverter on the CD40106 you already have.
The AND gate is what makes it a game. While the dot is spinning, FROZEN is 0, so both verdict gates output 0 no matter where the dot is — the green lamp cannot flicker as the gold light rushes past. The verdict only exists once you have committed. An AND gate used this way is a valve: one input is the signal, the other is permission.

The reason one light gets a transistor and nine don't

A CD4000 output at 5 V can push about 2½ milliamps into an LED before its own voltage starts sagging — down to about 2.5 V. For nine of the ten lights that's fine: their only job is to glow, and a sagging 2.5 V still glows.

But the gold light's output has a second job. Two chips have to read it and believe it. And the datasheet says an input only counts as a guaranteed 1 above 3.5 V. A wire sitting at 2.5 V is in the no-man's-land between 0 and 1, where a chip is allowed to believe whatever it likes.

So the gold light gets a PN2222 transistor and a 10 kΩ resistor. The chip now pushes only 0.4 mA — barely a load, so its output stays up near 4.6 V and reads as a clean 1 — and the transistor does the shoving instead. As a bonus the gold light ends up the brightest lamp in the ring, which is exactly what you want from a target.

This is the most useful thing in this guide. "It worked until I connected something else to it" is one of the most common faults in real electronics, and this is why: every wire has a strength as well as a value. Chips think, transistors shove — and if a signal has to be both bright and believed, it needs help.

Power

4.5 V to 5 V. Three AA cells, or the MB102 breadboard module set to 5 V. All four chips are happy from 3 V to 18 V. Nothing here goes near mains electricity.

About the ring's brightness. Nine of the lamps run at about 2½ mA, and each is only lit a tenth of the time. In a bright room the ring looks faint. Turn the lights down and it looks like an arcade machine — which, conveniently, is when you want to play it anyway.
Section 6

Six rules for not breaking things

Rule 2 has a new trap in it this time. Read it twice.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every single time.
  2. Count the pins before you trust them Three chips here are 14-pin, with + on 14 and − on 7. The CD4017 is 16-pin, with + on 16 and − on 8. Wire it like a 14-pin chip and you put 5 V into an output. Notch to the left, pin 1 bottom-left, numbers run anticlockwise.
  3. Never leave an input dangling Including the ones on halves of chips you aren't using. Every unused input goes to −. A floating CMOS input picks up radio noise and flickers.
  4. Never wire an output straight to + or − That's a short circuit through the chip. The CD4017 has ten outputs in a row and it is easy to ground one by accident while grounding its neighbours' inputs.
  5. Electrolytic capacitors have a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, they can pop. Both the 100 µF and the 10 µF have stripes; the little "104" ceramics don't care.
  6. Warm chip means stop Pull the power immediately, then re-check rules 2 and 4.
Section 7

The four pinouts

Two of these you've seen. Two are new, and one of them is a different shape.

If you need reminding how pin numbering and breadboard rows work, that's covered in detail in Project 01 — this guide assumes you've placed a chip before.

The CD4017's outputs are not in counting order. Count 0 comes out of pin 3, count 1 out of pin 2, count 2 out of pin 4. If you wire the LEDs in pin order the dot will hop around the ring in a jumble instead of walking. Stage 2 has the translation table; use it and don't improvise.
Stage 1The heartbeat ~25 min

Make time out of a resistor

One chip, two components, one blinking light. Nothing else works until this does.

Power the rails first: supply + to the top red rail, supply − to the top blue rail, then jumper top red to bottom red and top blue to bottom blue. Put the 100 µF capacitor across the rails with its stripe to −.

Now place the CD40106 across the channel, notch to the left, and wire it:

FromToWhy
pin 14+ railChip power
pin 7− railChip ground
0.1 µF ("104")pin 14 → pin 7Steadies the supply
100 kΩpin 2 → pin 1The timing resistor — output back to input
10 µF + legpin 1The timing capacitor
10 µF stripe leg− railPolarity matters here
pin 21 kΩ → LED long legSo you can see the beat
LED short leg− railCompletes the circuit
pins 5, 9, 11, 13− railUnused inputs — rule 3

Leave pins 3, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 empty for now. Pin 3 gets used in Stage 4, and the rest are outputs you'll never need — don't ground them.

Power up. The LED should blink about once a second, steadily, forever.

  • Count the blinks in 20 seconds and write it in the worksheet. You should get somewhere near 25.
  • Pull the 100 kΩ out and put a 1 MΩ in its place. Ten times the resistance, so a tenth of the speed — one blink every eight seconds. Painfully slow. Put the 100 kΩ back.
  • Now swap the 10 µF capacitor for the little 0.1 µF "104" — a hundred times smaller. The LED stops looking like a blink and starts looking like a dim, steady glow, because it is now flickering about 120 times a second, far too fast for your eye.
That last one is worth sitting with. Nothing changed except how big the bucket is. Blinking, flickering and "sounds like a note" are the same circuit at different speeds — and that's the whole of Project 05, when we get to it.

Finish the stage with the game clock in place: 1 MΩ and the 0.1 µF "104". About 12 beats a second. The LED will look like a steady dim glow — that's correct, and the ring will show you it's really ticking.

What you just built has a name. A relaxation oscillator. Before quartz crystals were cheap this is how clocks, blinkers, metronomes and organ keyboards were made, and it's still how most blinking indicator lights work. It has no program, no crystal and no instructions — just a bucket, a pipe, and something that changes its mind at two different voltages.
Stage 2The ring ~40 min

Ten lights, one at a time

The longest stage, and the one that looks like something.

Before you wire ten LEDs, put the clock back into slow mode — 100 kΩ and the 10 µF. At one step per second you can watch the dot arrive at each lamp and check your work as you go. At game speed you'd never see which one was wrong.

The CD4017 is a 16-pin chip. + goes to pin 16 and − to pin 8. Not 14 and 7. Check it twice before you switch on.

Place the CD4017, notch to the left, and wire the control pins:

Arrange them in a circle if your jumper wires will reach, or a straight row if they won't — the circuit doesn't care, and a row is easier to build. Put the yellow one at position 6 either way, so it's in the middle of a row and not at an end where it would be easier to hit.

Power up. One lamp at a time should light, walking from position 1 to position 10 and back to 1, about one step per second.

  • Watch it go round twice and check the dot never jumps or skips.
  • If it hops around in a jumble, you wired the LEDs in pin order instead of using the table.
  • If one lamp stays dark, it's backwards — long leg toward the chip.
  • Now switch to game speed: 1 MΩ and the 0.1 µF. The dot becomes a spinning blur.
Why the pins are in that strange order. Inside, the CD4017 isn't ten separate things — it's five flip-flops in a loop, and the chip designers laid the pins out to keep the internal wiring short and cheap, not to be convenient for you. Datasheets are full of decisions like this. It's a good early lesson that the outside of a part is shaped by its insides, and that reading the drawing beats guessing.
Stage 3The freeze ~25 min

A circuit that remembers

After this stage it's already playable — just without a referee.

A push button is only pressed for a tenth of a second, and the dot is moving. We need the machine to hold on to the fact that you pressed. That's what a flip-flop is for: two inputs called SET and RESET, and one output that stays wherever it was last put.

Place the CD4013 — back to 14 pins, + on 14 and − on 7.

Hand the freeze to the counter

Now remove the temporary wire from CD4017 pin 13 to −, and put this in its place:

FromToWhy
CD4013 pin 1 (Q1)CD4017 pin 13 FROZEN → clock inhibit

Power up and press RESET first. The dot should spin. Press STOP and it should stop dead, and stay stopped however long you wait. Press RESET and it carries on from where it stopped.

Press RESET after every power-up. A flip-flop wakes up in whichever state it feels like — that's what makes it memory, and nobody told it what to remember before it was switched on. If the ring is frozen the moment you power up, it isn't broken; it just woke up remembering a press that never happened.
You have built one bit of RAM. A gigabyte of memory is this circuit about eight billion times over, shrunk. The clock-inhibit pin is worth noticing too: the chip designers knew people would want to stop a counter without losing its count, so they gave it a pin for exactly that. Real parts are full of thoughtfulness like this, and it's all in the datasheet.

This is what "not enough drive" looks like in real life: not a dead circuit, but a moody one.
Stage 5Tune it ~15 min

Make it as hard as you can stand

One resistor decides whether this is a toy or a challenge.

The clock resistor is between CD40106 pin 2 and pin 1. To speed the game up, plug more 1 MΩ resistors into those same two holes, side by side. More pipes, less resistance, faster clock.

1 MΩ resistorsBehaves like Beats/secTimes round per secondVerdict
11 MΩ≈ 121.2 You'll win about a third of the time once you learn it
2 side by side500 kΩ≈ 25 2.5Properly hard
3 side by side333 kΩ≈ 37 3.7Mostly luck
4 side by side250 kΩ≈ 49 4.9Entirely luck. Good for tournaments

Now play it properly. Ten rounds each, one press per round, and keep score in the worksheet — there's a table there that turns your hit rate into an estimate of your own reaction time, which is a genuinely interesting number to know about yourself.

You will get better at this, and not by reacting faster. Your reaction time is roughly fixed at about a quarter of a second — far longer than one step of the ring. Winning means learning to press before the dot arrives, by about three lamps. You're not beating the machine, you're predicting it. That's the same trick a drummer uses.
BonusThree upgrades ~20 min each

If you want to keep going

Keep score in hardware

WIN goes high and stays high until RESET, which means it makes exactly one clean edge per victory. Feed it into a second CD4017's clock (pin 14, with pin 13 and pin 15 to −) and wire ten blue LEDs to its outputs using the same table as Stage 2. Now the machine counts your wins up to nine, in hardware, with no code anywhere. First to light the fifth lamp is the champion.

Let the room set the difficulty

Take the 1 MΩ clock resistor out and put the photoresistor in its place. Its resistance drops as light hits it, so the game gets faster in bright light and slower in shadow — and you can control the speed by cupping your hand over it. Play against someone who is allowed to adjust the lighting.

Nine steps for a run of 90

CD4017 pin 12 is carry out: it pulses once every ten counts. Feed it into another CD4017's clock and the second chip counts tens while the first counts ones — which is exactly how a two-digit counter works, and how the odometer in a car is arranged. You now have 100 positions. Making a game out of that is up to you.

When it doesn't work

Nearly always one of these. Full table in README.md.

What you seeAlmost always
Nothing anywherePower not reaching a chip. Remember the CD4017 wants + on 16 and − on 8.
A chip gets warmPower in backwards, or an output grounded. Unplug now.
Clock LED doesn't blinkThe resistor must go from pin 2 back to pin 1. If it goes to a rail instead, there's no feedback and no oscillation.
