Section 2

How chips make music

The three blocks inside every analog sequencer.

Before microprocessors existed, electronic musicians made songs with analog sequencers. Famous instruments like the Roland TB-303 (the sound of acid house) and the Moog modular sequencer relied on three simple functional blocks:

  1. The Clock (Tempo): A slow oscillator (1 to 10 Hz) ticking steadily like a metronome. In our circuit, one inverter of the CD40106 generates this heartbeat.
  2. The Distributor (Step Counter): A CD4017 decade counter that receives each clock pulse and advances a single HIGH output along eight pins in order (0 → 7).
  3. The Voice (Pitch & Gating): A fast oscillator (100 to 1,000 Hz) whose frequency depends on a timing resistor. The active step feeds current through its trim pot, commanding the oscillator to sing that note. A CD4066 bilateral switch acts as an electronic gate, opening only during the beat so each note hits cleanly.
Why this is called an "analog-digital hybrid". The clock and counter are strictly digital — 1s and 0s counting in binary sequence. But the pitch network is purely analog: a continuous range of resistance steering continuous voltage. Combining digital timing with analog tone is the foundation of electronic music.
Section 3

The 16-pin trap & modulo-8 reset

Why the CD4017 is different from every other chip in this lab.

The CD4017 is the only chip here with 16 pins. Almost every logic chip puts power on pin 14 and ground on pin 7. The CD4017 puts + on pin 16 and − on pin 8. If you treat it like a 14-pin chip, pin 14 gets +5 V — which connects supply power straight into the clock input, freezing the counter dead.

Making an 8-step loop

The CD4017 is designed to count to ten (Q0 through Q9). We want an 8-step musical loop (steps 0 to 7). How do we make a 10-step counter stop at 8? We connect output Q8 (pin 9) straight to RESET (pin 15)!

When the 8th beat ends, the chip switches to count 8 for roughly 100 nanoseconds — just long enough for pin 9 to pull pin 15 HIGH, instantaneously snapping the counter back to count 0. To human ears, the reset is completely invisible: you hear a seamless, looping 8-step melody.

The 4-step variation. Want a short 4-step techno loop? Disconnect pin 15 from pin 9 and connect it to pin 10 (Q4) instead. Now the counter resets on step 4, looping steps 0, 1, 2, 3 endlessly.

Stage 3Making Sound (The Audio Voice) ~30 min

Build the pitch oscillator and speaker driver

A fast Schmitt inverter and an NPN transistor that vibrates an 8 Ω speaker.

Now we turn electricity into sound. Inverter 2 on the CD40106 (pins 3 and 4) will oscillate at hundreds of cycles per second — audible audio frequency. A PN2222 transistor amplifies the weak CMOS output to drive your passive speaker.

Do not omit the 100 Ω resistor. Without it, the speaker coil draws over 300 mA, which will scorch your transistor and drop the battery voltage so low that the logic chips reset. The 100 Ω keeps the volume pleasant and the transistor cool.

Test Stage 3

Power on. You should hear a steady buzzing tone through the speaker. Touch a 10 kΩ resistor in parallel across the 47 kΩ — the pitch jumps higher! Remove the temporary 47 kΩ resistor when verified.

Why a square wave sounds buzzy. A pure musical flute note is a smooth sine wave. A logic inverter output can only be full ON or full OFF, creating a sharp square wave. Square waves contain strong odd harmonics — the same rich, buzzy tone heard in 8-bit Nintendo game soundtracks and distorted synthesizer leads.
Stage 4Tuning the Melody (The 8 Pots) ~45 min

Eight trim pots meet on a common pitch bus

How digital step outputs steer analog resistance without colliding.

We want each step to set its own pitch. But if all eight outputs were wired together, they would short-circuit each other! To solve this, each step's trim pot wiper passes through an isolation diode (1N4148) before joining the common Pitch Bus.

Only the currently active step is at 5 V; the other seven are at 0 V. The diodes allow current to flow *only* from the active step into the pitch bus, while blocking current from leaking backwards into the seven inactive steps!

Wiring the 8 pitch pots

For each step 1 through 8:

  1. Connect the pot's left leg to the CD4017 step output pin.
  2. Connect the pot's middle wiper to the anode of a 1N4148 diode.
  3. Connect the diode's cathode (marked with a black stripe) to a common breadboard row: the Pitch Bus.
  4. Connect the Pitch Bus directly to CD40106 pin 3 (the audio timing node).

Test Stage 4

Power on! As the LEDs chase 1 through 8, the speaker plays an 8-note sequence. Take a small screwdriver or use your fingers to turn the trim pots: you can tune each note individually to compose your own musical melody!

Control Voltage (CV). In 1964, Bob Moog revolutionized music by using variable voltages to control audio pitch. Before digital protocols like MIDI existed, synthesizers were connected using "CV and Gate" patch cables — the exact principle you just built with potentiometers and diodes.
Stage 5Analog Gating & Slide (CD4066) ~35 min

The quad bilateral switch adds rhythm and glide

Turn continuous drone into punchy staccato bass and classic acid slides.

Right now, your sequencer sounds like a siren — the pitch slides continuously from note to note without stopping. Real music has rhythm: space between the notes. We will use the CD4066 bilateral switch to gate the audio on and off with the clock, and add an analog portamento slide.

  • With Gate active: The notes no longer slur together. Each step punches with a crisp, rhythmic attack, followed by silence before the next note.
  • Engage Slide (pin 6 to +): The capacitor slows down how fast the voltage changes between steps. Notes glide smoothly into each other like a trombone or an acid bass synth!
The Roland TB-303 and acid house. In 1982, Roland released the TB-303 Bass Line. It was a commercial flop until Chicago musicians in the late 1980s discovered that cranking the tempo and switching between staccato gates and analog slides produced a wild, squelchy groove. That single combination of a step counter, bilateral gate, and slide capacitor birthed acid house music.
Section 4

When it doesn't work

Three chips and eight potentiometers give plenty of places for loose wires.

What you hear or seeAlmost always
Nothing lights and no soundPower not reaching the chips. Remember the CD4017 wants + on pin 16 and − on pin 8.
Steady high-pitched squeal that never stepsTempo clock is not oscillating. Check the 1 µF capacitor and 100 kΩ pot on CD40106 pins 1 and 2.
LEDs chase, but pitch never changesDiodes are in backwards or missing. The black stripe must face the common Pitch Bus.
Counter stops on step 8 and freezesPin 9 (Q8) is not reaching pin 15 (RESET), or pin 13 (CLOCK INHIBIT) is floating instead of grounded.
Notes drone continuously without rhythmic gapsCD4066 pin 13 is stuck HIGH. Connect pin 13 to clock pin 2 through the 10 kΩ pull-down.
Buzzer is silent, but LEDs sequence normallyActive buzzer used instead of passive (test across 5 V battery), or PN2222 transistor is backwards (flat side faces you: E-B-C).
Transistor or chips feel warmThe mandatory 100 Ω speaker resistor was omitted. Unplug immediately and insert the 100 Ω.
Melody steps in a confusing, jumbled orderThe CD4017 outputs are scrambled on the physical package. Follow the pin order table: 3, 2, 4, 7, 10, 1, 5, 6.
