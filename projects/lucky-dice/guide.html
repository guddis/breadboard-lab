Shift registers & pseudo-random numbers · Project 09

Lucky
Dice

Hold a button and seven LEDs flicker through the faces of a die. Let go and it stops on one. There is no processor and no random-number chip — three flip-flops pass a bit along a row, and a single gate decides what the next bit is. The surprise is how random something that simple can look.

Section 1

What you're building

Roll it here first. Hold the button for as long as you like.

Hold ROLL and the die flickers. Let go and it stops. Do that thirty times and look at the tally underneath: every face turns up about equally often, and so does the seventh outcome — a dark die. Section 8 explains where that came from and why it is not a bug.

Turn Slow clock on and hold ROLL to watch one bit move per second. Power on starts the chips in a random state, the way a real board wakes up — one of the eight possibilities is a trap, and RESET is the way out.

The whole idea in one line. A row of three one-bit memories where every tick each one copies its neighbour, and the newest bit is made by comparing the other two. That is a linear-feedback shift register, and it is inside Wi-Fi, GPS and most things that need a stream of bits that looks like noise.
Section 2

How three bits make seven steps

A row of memories, and one gate that feeds the front of it.

The shift register

Each D flip-flop is the one-bit memory from Project 03. Wire three in a row, give them all the same clock, and on every tick each one copies the bit from the one before it. A bit placed at the front marches to the right and drops off the end.

The feedback

If the front just took whatever you gave it, the pattern would march out and leave nothing behind. Instead the front takes XNOR(Q2, Q3): 1 when the last two bits are the same, 0 when they differ. The row now feeds itself.

Start with everything at 0 and tick. This table is not typed in — the page computed it from the same functions the simulator uses:

TickQ1Q2Q3 XNOR(Q2,Q3) = next Q1Die

Seven different states, then back to the start. It visits all of them exactly once — that is the longest cycle three bits can make with one tap pair (all eight states minus one trap, which Section 3 is about). Notice what it does not do: it does not count. The sequence 100, 110, 011, 101, 010, 001 jumps about, and that jumping is what makes it look random.

What this is really for. Wi-Fi scrambles every packet with a 7-bit version of this circuit, so a long run of zeros in your data does not become a long silence on the radio. Each GPS satellite has its own 10-bit register that repeats every 1 023 steps — the receiver recognises the satellite by its pattern. Same parts, same idea, a few more flip-flops.
Section 3

XNOR, and the one state it can't leave

Why this project uses XNOR and not the more famous XOR.

Three bits have eight states. The cycle above uses seven. The eighth is a trap — a state that feeds itself and never leaves. Which state is the trap depends on which gate you pick, and that matters because RESET puts the register at 000:

Feedback gateStartAfter 1 tick After 20 ticksWhat it means

With XOR, 000 is the trap. RESET would put you straight into it, and the die would sit dark for ever. With XNOR, the trap moves to 111 — and you cannot get there from RESET. You can only start in it by switching on at an unlucky moment, because a flip-flop wakes up holding whatever it likes.

Press RESET after every power-up. If all seven pips are lit and ROLL does nothing, you powered on inside the trap. That is not a fault; it is this section, happening to you. RESET fixes it.

In Stage 3 you will swap the XNOR chip for an XOR chip, in the same holes, and watch RESET trap the die. It takes thirty seconds and nobody forgets it.

Section 4

From three bits to seven lamps

The die's faces are handed out so the decoder needs only two gates.

A die face is a pattern of up to seven pips. Wire each state to the lamps that make its face and you get a decoder. The tidy trick: pips on a die come in opposite pairs, so seven lamps need only four signals.

Stateab cCFace
a
top-left and bottom-right   = Q2 OR Q3  — one OR gate
b
top-right and bottom-left   = Q3  — a wire
c
middle-left and middle-right = Q2 AND Q3  — one AND gate
C
centre   = Q1  — a wire

The order of faces — 1, 3, 6, 5, 2, 4 — was not chosen by taste. There are 720 ways to hand six faces to six states. Checking all of them takes a computer less than a second, and this one is the cheapest: two gates and two wires for the whole decoder.

Where you've seen this. That search — try every assignment, keep the one that needs the least hardware — is what chip designers call state assignment, and design tools do it millions of times when they lay out a processor. Project 07 did the same by hand for a seven-segment display.

Section 6

Rules for not breaking things

The same as before, with a new one at number 4.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every single time.
  2. All six chips are 14-pin: + on pin 14, − on pin 7 Notch to the left, pin 1 bottom-left, numbers run anticlockwise. There is no 16-pin chip in this project.
  3. Never leave an input dangling That includes the SET and RESET pins you are not using, and every spare gate input. Spare inputs go to −.
  4. Press RESET after every power-up Flip-flops wake up holding anything, and one of the eight possibilities is the trap from Section 3.
  5. Never wire an output straight to + or − That is a short through the chip.
  6. The electrolytic capacitors have a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, they can pop.
  7. Touch something metal before handling chips Static kills CMOS.
  8. Warm chip means stop Pull the power and re-check rule 2.
Section 7

The pinouts

Pin numbering and breadboard rows are in Project 01 if you need reminding.

The CD4070 you swap in during Stage 3 has exactly the CD4077's pinout.

A CD4013 flip-flop has two halves with different pin numbers. Half 1 uses pins 1–6; half 2 uses 8–13. You use half 1 of both chips and half 2 of the first. The second chip's half 2 is idle — tie its four inputs to − rather than leave them in the air.
Stage 1The clock~20 min

Make time pass

The same trick as Project 03: an inverter fed from its own output.

Supply + to the top red rail, supply − to the top blue rail, then jumper across to the bottom rails. Put the 100 µF across the rails, stripe to −.

Place the CD4093, notch left. Use gate 1; tie its two inputs together so it works as a NOT:

Power on.

  • The lamp blinks about once a second: on, off, on, off.
  • f ≈ 1.23 ÷ (R × C) = 1.23 ÷ (100 000 × 0.000 010) ≈ 1.2 blinks per second. Yours may be half as fast or half as quick again — electrolytic capacitors are loose by 20 % or more, and the switching point of each chip differs. Nothing is wrong.
The lamp is dim on purpose. A CD4000 output only gives a milliamp or two. The 2 kΩ keeps the output above 4 V so the clock stays a clean 0 or 1 — a brighter lamp would pull it down to a vague voltage and spoil the timing.
What this is really for. Every digital machine has a clock, and the clock is the only reason anything happens. Yours is an inverter that keeps disagreeing with itself, slowed down by a resistor filling a capacitor.

Power on, press RESET, then hold ROLL.

  • One lamp after another comes on, left to right, one per blink. After three blinks all three are lit and stay lit.

The lamps are dim, and that is the 2 kΩ. Each Q also feeds the next flip-flop, which needs a clean 1. Wiring a bright lamp straight on would drag the output to a vague 2.5 V that the next chip may not read as 1. Project 03 found this out the hard way.
What this is really for. A shift register turns a stream of bits arriving one at a time into a row of bits available all at once. Keyboards, memory chips and the serial port on every computer use one to do exactly that.

  1. Power off. Pull the CD4077 and put a CD4070 in the same holes Same pinout, notch the same way round. It is an XOR instead of an XNOR.
  2. Power on, RESET, hold ROLL Nothing happens. All three lamps stay dark for as long as you like.

Why a transistor, when the test lamps managed without. Each lamp now draws about 8 mA and two share a driver. A CD4000 output can give a milliamp or so, and Q1, Q2 and Q3 also have to feed the logic. The 10 kΩ into the base asks the output for 0.4 mA — so light for the pips, a clean 1 for the logic.

Power on, press RESET, hold ROLL.

  • The pips flicker through the faces. At this speed — one step a second — you can read each.
  • Let go: a face stays. About one time in seven the die is dark — that is the seventh state, not a fault.
  • RESET gives a dark die; the first six ticks after it are 1, 3, 6, 5, 2, 4.

Now make it fast

Power off. Swap the 10 µF for a 0.1 µF in the same two holes. The clock is now f ≈ 1.23 ÷ (100 000 × 0.000 000 1) ≈ 120 ticks a second, which is 17 full trips round the seven states every second. Hold ROLL and the die becomes a blur; let go and you cannot have aimed.

What this is really for. You just did what a game console does when it needs a random number and has no random-number hardware: run a small register fast, and read it when something unpredictable — here, your thumb — says stop.
BonusIf you want moreany order

A "roll again" lamp, and a longer register

The roll-again lamp

The dark die is the only state where Q1, Q2 and Q3 are all 0. That is NOT(Q1 OR Q2 OR Q3). The CD4071 already has a = Q2 OR Q3, so one more OR gate finishes it: move CD4071 pins 5 and 6 off − and put Q1 (A pin 1) on pin 5 and a (CD4071 pin 3) on pin 6. Pin 4 is now 1 for every state except dark. Drive a fifth transistor from it the same way as the others, with a green LED: lit means "valid roll".

Four bits instead of three

The idle half of chip B is the fourth flip-flop: B pin 9 (D2) to B pin 1 (Q3) instead of −, B pin 11 to CLK, B pin 10 to the RESET node; its output is B pin 13. Move the XNOR inputs to Q3 and Q4 (B pin 1 and B pin 13). The cycle is now 15 states instead of 7. The die decoder no longer means anything — put test lamps on all four and count.

Section 8

Is it fair?

Seven equally likely outcomes, and a die with six faces.

Seven states are visited in turn, and nothing makes one more likely than another. What matters is when you let go. A single trip round the cycle lasts 1 ÷ 17 of a second, about 57 ms. Your thumb does not release the button to within 57 ms of a plan; it wanders by hundreds. So every state is equally likely, and the dark one turns up one roll in seven.

You cannot get six equally likely outcomes out of seven by any trick — seven does not divide into six. What you can do is throw one away and roll again, which is what the dark die is. Statisticians call it rejection sampling and it is how fair random numbers are made from unfair or awkward sources everywhere.

It is pseudo-random, and honest about it. From RESET the register always goes 1, 3, 6, 5, 2, 4. If you could count 3 ticks at the slow clock you could roll a six every time — the worksheet has you do it. At 120 ticks a second, nobody can. "Random" in electronics nearly always means "too fast for the person using it to steer".

The worksheet has a 60-roll tally table to test it on your own board.
