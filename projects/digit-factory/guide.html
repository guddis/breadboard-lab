Decoding, from scratch · Project 07

The Digit
Factory

Three switches set a number in binary. Nineteen logic gates argue about it for a hundred-millionth of a second, and a digit you can read appears on the display. The Score Tower had a chip that did this for you and never showed its working. This is the working.

Age 12+, and it helps to have built one project before this Time about 2½ hours, in 4 stages Voltage 5 V — nothing dangerous Chips CD4081 × 2, CD4071 × 2, CD4070, CD4069
Section 1

What you're building

Flip the three switches. The digit is worked out by gates you are about to wire, one at a time.

No counter, no decoder chip, no clever 16-pin part. Three inputs, six ordinary gate chips from the Gate Bench, and a seven-segment display that ends up spelling a number.

An orange dot means that gate is holding its segment at 5 V, which on this display means dark. The bars that light are the ones the gates pull down to −. That sounds backwards, and Section 3 is about why it is the cheaper way round.

The whole project in one line. A digit is not one answer. It is seven separate yes-or-no answers about the same three switches — and every one of them is a logic equation you can write down, minimise, and build.
Section 2

Three switches make eight numbers

Binary, with your hands on it.

Each switch is worth a number: A is 1, B is 2, C is 4. A switch in the + rail counts; a switch in − does not. Add up the ones that count and that is the number on the display.

Eight rows, because three switches can be arranged eight ways — 2 × 2 × 2. Add a fourth switch and you get sixteen; that is the whole idea of binary and it is the last time this guide will explain it.

Your display cannot show an 8. Three bits stop at seven. Counting in eights like this has a name — octal — and it was how programmers wrote numbers down for about twenty years, because three switches, three lamps and three holes in a punched card all line up neatly.

The font is not yours to choose

Eight digits, each one a set of bars. This is the same font the CD4026 drew in the Score Tower, and for the same reason: it is what a seven-segment display can do.

Read that table by column instead of by row. The a column is the complete specification of one wire: a is lit for 0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 and dark for 1 and 4. Nothing else about the project matters to segment a.

Seven small problems beat one big one. "Turn a number into a digit" is hard to think about. "When is the top bar dark?" is almost easy. Splitting one wide problem into columns you can solve separately is the move, and it is the same move whether you are wiring gates or writing a program.
Section 3

Work out the darkness, not the light

The trick that makes this project fit in six chips instead of eight.

Your display is common anode: all seven bars have their + legs joined to the + rail, so a bar lights when something pulls its other end down to −. A 0 lights a bar. A 1 leaves it dark.

In the Score Tower that was bad news — the CD4026 says 1 for a bar it wants lit, so fourteen inverters had to go in to flip every answer. Here, nobody has decided anything yet. You are designing the logic, so you can design it the way the display already wants.

So do not ask "which bars light?". Ask "which bars stay dark?" — and get a gate to answer that. The answer is a 1, the display reads it as dark, and no inverter is needed anywhere.

It is also less work, and here is the proof. Count the entries in the font table:

Thirty-six lightings against twenty darknesses. Asking the sparser question means fewer cases to build, and fewer cases means fewer gates. Segment c is the extreme: it is dark for exactly one of the eight digits, so its whole equation is "the number is 2".

The same circuit, priced both ways. Compute the light and you need the gates plus seven inverters — two more CD4069s, fourteen more wires, one more afternoon. Press the Design button in the simulator to switch between them: the digit is identical and the chip count is not. This is what engineers mean by an active-low signal, and it is why so many real chips have a line over their pin names.
Nobody adds an inverter if they can move the question. Reset pins that reset when low, chip-select pins that select when low, error flags that mean trouble when low — all of them are somebody noticing which polarity was free. It costs nothing at design time and a whole chip afterwards.
Section 4

Seven equations, and where they come from

This is the paperwork. Do it at the table, with a pencil, before the breadboard comes out.

Take segment a, the top bar. From the font table it is dark for 1 and 4, and lit for the other six. Write those two digits out in binary:

dark forCB A
100 1
410 0

Two things are true of both rows, and of no other row in the table. B is 0. And A and C disagree — one of them is 1 and the other is 0. That second one is an XOR, which you met in the Gate Bench as the gate that means "these two are different".

So: the top bar is dark when B is 0 and A differs from C. Three gates.

Do that six more times and the project is designed. Here is every answer, and you should check two or three of them against the font table yourself rather than trusting the page:

Notice what gets shared. That single B̄ inverter feeds four different gates, and its answer ends up inside five of the seven segments. A·B·C — "the number is 7" — is used by two. A gate output can feed as many inputs as you like, so a signal you have already built is free: nineteen gates cover all seven segments, where building each one on its own would take twenty-nine gates and eight chips.

Where the gates go

Nineteen gates, six chips, and the packages come in fours. Two chips end up completely full, which is satisfying and also a warning: there is no spare AND gate to make a mistake with.

This is logic synthesis, done by hand. Truth table in, gates out. A chip designer today writes the truth table in a language called Verilog and a program does the rest — the program searches for the shared terms and the cheap polarity the same way you just did, for a few million gates instead of nineteen. The step is called synthesis, and it is the same step.

The segments will be dimmer than the Score Tower's, and the resistor is not why. A CD4081 output can pull down about 1 mA at 5 V — the datasheet guarantees 0.64 mA and typically gives 1 mA. That, not your 220 Ω, is what sets the brightness: the chip runs out of push long before the resistor starts limiting anything. It is readable indoors and disappointing in sunlight.
The knob to turn if it is too dim. Run the whole board at 9 V instead of 5 V — two 4.5 V battery packs in series, or a 9 V block. These chips are specified to 18 V, and a CD4081 at 10 V pulls down about 2.6 mA instead of 1 mA. The digit gets noticeably brighter and nothing else in the circuit changes. Keep the 220 Ω resistors in: they do nothing at 5 V and they are your insurance at 9 V.
Section 6

Rules for not breaking things

Six chips is more than you have had on one board before. The rules matter more, not less.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every time. You will be at this board for hours.
  2. Every chip here is + on 14 and − on 7 All six of them, no exceptions. This is the first project in the box where that is true of everything, so enjoy it.
  3. Never leave an input dangling A floating CMOS input is not 0 — it drifts, and the gate warms up. Every unused input on every chip goes to −, and there is a table of exactly which ones in the README.
  4. Never wire an output to + or − That is a short through the chip. A gate output goes to gate inputs, or through a resistor to the display, and nowhere else.
  5. One output can feed many inputs B̄ — the output of one inverter — goes to four different gates. That is normal and costs nothing. Two outputs into one node is the thing that is never allowed.
  6. Label as you go Write the signal name on a scrap of tape beside each row you use. By Stage 3 you will have nineteen named signals and no memory of which is which.
  7. Warm chip means stop Pull the power. Check rule 2, then rule 4.
The one habit that saves this build. Wire one gate, then power up and check that one gate with the input switches before wiring the next. Nineteen gates checked one at a time takes twenty minutes. Nineteen gates checked at the end takes an evening.

Why the pull-down resistors are not optional. Pull a jumper out entirely. The lamp goes out and the row reads 0, because the 100 kΩ is holding it down. Without it that row would read whatever the air nearby feels like, your finger would change the digit, and Stage 2 would be unbuildable. This is the same lesson as the Gate Bench's Stage 5, and it is the one that keeps coming back.

Take a wire from the − rail and touch the free end of each resistor. One bar lights per resistor. Touch a, b and c together and you have a 7.

  • All seven bars light, one at a time. A bar that never lights is a resistor in the wrong row, or an LED-shaped assumption about which pin is which.
  • Write your segment-to-pin map in the worksheet now. Every table from here on refers to it.
  • Leave the seven resistors in place. Their free ends are where Stage 2 plugs in.
Test the ends, then trust them. When a long chain fails, the fault is somewhere in the middle nine times out of ten — but only if you proved the ends. Ten minutes with a bare wire now buys you the right to say "the display is fine" for the rest of the build, and that is worth more than it sounds.

The shared signals, and segments a, b and c

Nine gates. Wire them in this order, top to bottom — every gate's inputs come from something already built above it.

Then the three outputs reach the display. These are the only three wires in the whole project that carry current rather than an opinion:

Before power: the unused inputs. The CD4069 has three spare inverters and the CD4070 two spare XORs, and their inputs are dangling right now. Tie CD4069 pins 9, 11 and 13 and CD4070 pins 8, 9, 12 and 13 to the − rail. Seven wires that do nothing except stop the chips from misbehaving.

Power up and work through all eight numbers. Three bars are under gate control and four are still dark, so what you should see is:

  • 1 and 7 are correct and finished. They only ever needed a, b and c.
  • 0, 3 and 7 look identical. They differ only in bars you have not wired yet.
  • Set 2 and check segment c goes dark. That single inverter is the whole c equation and the only segment that is right for all eight numbers already.
If one segment is wrong for exactly one number, you have a wire on the wrong pin of the right gate. If it is wrong for four numbers, one of its inputs is on the wrong row — A instead of B, usually. The number of wrong digits tells you how deep the mistake is.
Fan-out is free, and that is why chips look like this. B̄ leaves one inverter and arrives at four different gates. Copying a signal costs you nothing but a wire, while computing it again costs a gate — so real designs are full of one signal going to dozens of places. It is the reason a chip photograph looks more like a road map than a row of boxes.
Stage 3Seven gates, three more segments ~35 min

Now it can count to three

Segments d, e and g — and the first gate that reuses another gate's answer.

Two of these equations need to know whether the number is 7, so that gets built once, as A·B·C, and shared. Segment d needs the answer segment a already worked out, so its wire comes from a gate output rather than from a switch.

Power up. Six of the seven bars now work:

  • 1, 2, 3 and 7 are correct and finished.
  • 0, 4, 5 and 6 are all missing the same bar — the top left one. That is f, and it is the only thing left.
  • Set 7 and look at the d segment. It must be dark. If it lights, the wire from segment a's AND gate went to the wrong OR input.
Segment d is worth staring at. Its equation is "a is dark, or the number is 7". The bottom bar of a digit borrows the answer from the top bar of the same digit, which is not a fact about arithmetic — it is a coincidence in the shape of the font, and minimising the equation found it for you. This is what people mean when they say a machine found a solution nobody would have written.
Sharing is the whole economy of a chip. Nineteen gates instead of twenty-nine, because four signals get used more than once and segment d borrows segment a's answer outright. Every real design starts by hunting for the terms that appear in more than one output, and a synthesis program spends most of its time on exactly that search.
Stage 4The last three gates ~20 min

Four digits arrive at once

Segment f, the fiddliest equation in the project, and then it is a machine.

The top-left bar is dark for 1, 2, 3 and 7, and that set has no single neat description. It needs two ORs feeding an AND: A or B is set, and C is clear or the number is 7.

The last unused inputs. CD4081 #2 has one spare AND and CD4071 #2 one spare OR. Tie CD4081 #2 pins 12 and 13 and CD4071 #2 pins 12 and 13 to −. Now every input on all six chips goes somewhere.

Power up and count 0 to 7 on the switches.

Eight digits, nineteen gates, no counter, no decoder, no code. Every bar on that display is lit because an AND or an OR you wired by hand decided it should be.

  • Work through all eight numbers twice and compare against the font row above.
  • Fill in the last table in the worksheet — your own display, your own reading, not the picture on this page.
  • Wiggle the board. Nothing should change. If a digit flickers, an unused input is still floating somewhere.
You have just built the inside of a chip. The CD4511, the CD4026 and every seven-segment decoder ever sold contain this, drawn smaller: a truth table turned into gates, with the polarity chosen to suit the display and the shared terms factored out. A CD4511 costs about the same as one of your CD4081s and does the whole job, which is exactly the trade the industry made. The difference is that you can point at yours and say what each gate is for.
BonusFour directionsas long as you like

If it is going to live on the shelf

  1. Make it count by itself Three switches are three bits, and three flip-flops are also three bits. Borrow the two CD4013s from the Quiz Buzzer, wire each one as a toggle (D to its own Q̄), clock the first from a button and each later one from the previous stage's Q̄, and feed Q into your A, B and C rows. That is a binary counter, and a counter plus your decoder is a CD4026.
  2. Add the fourth bit and find out why nobody does this by hand A fourth switch takes you to fifteen, so every one of the seven equations has to be re-derived over sixteen rows instead of eight. Work out segment a only, and count the gates it needs now. Then look up the CD4511 and notice that it is one chip.
  3. Put a hex digit on it Four bits reach 15, and the usual answer is to show A, b, C, d, E, F for 10 to 15 — which is what a memory dump looks like. Same seven bars, six more rows in the font table, all of them your choice this time.
  4. Race the Score Tower Feed the same three bits into both boards — yours and a CD4026 — and look for the moment they disagree. There is one, about half a microsecond wide. A CD4071 takes about 125 ns to answer at 5 V (the datasheet's typical figure, and it drops to 60 ns at 10 V), and segment c is four gates deep — inverter, OR, OR, inverter. Depth, not gate count, is what makes logic slow, which is why the equations worth minimising are the deep ones.
Section 8

When it doesn't work

Nineteen gates fail in about six ways. These are them.

What you seeAlmost always
One bar is wrong for exactly one numberA wire on the wrong pin of the right gate. Check that segment's output pin against the table — 3, 4, 10 and 11 are easy to confuse.
One bar is wrong for four numbersOne of its inputs is on the wrong input row. A instead of B, or C instead of C̄.
One bar is on for everythingIt is wired to + through something, or its gate's input is floating. Follow it back one gate at a time.
One bar never lights at allProve the segment with the Stage 1 bare-wire test first. That splits the problem in half in ten seconds.
Every bar is the photo-negative of the right answerThe display's common pin is on − instead of +. One wire, seven wrong bars.
The digit changes when you move your hand near the boardA floating input. Check the two warning boxes in Stages 2 and 4 — there are eleven pins that must go to −.
Everything is dimNormal. See the parts section: about 1 mA a segment at 5 V. Run the board at 9 V if it bothers you.
Two segments change together when only one shouldTwo outputs are in the same breadboard row. Find them and move one — that is a short between two chips.
A chip gets warmPower in backwards, or an output wired to a rail. Unplug now.
Everything is wrong at onceUnplug, walk away five minutes, re-read one table. This is what real engineers do.
The technique that finds gate faults fastest. Do not follow the wires. Set the switches to a number that is wrong, then check each gate on that segment's chain in turn: is this gate's output what its own two inputs say it should be? The first gate that disagrees with its own inputs is the fault. Three checks, not nineteen.
