What you're building

A scoreboard. One button adds one, one button wipes it back to zero, and it holds the number until you change it. Keep it next to the Quiz Buzzer and you have a quiz.

The whole idea in one line. A chip counts 0 to 9 and, instead of handing you the number in binary, hands you seven separate answers — one per bar of the digit. That translation has a name: decoding.
Section 2

Seven bars, ten numbers

Why a calculator digit looks the way it does.

A seven-segment display is seven LEDs in one lump of plastic, arranged in the shape of an 8. They have names, and the names are always the same on every display ever made — clockwise from the top bar: a, b, c, d, e, f, and then g across the middle.

Ten digits, each one a different set of those seven. This is the entire font:

Look at the 9. It has no bar along the bottom. Look at the 6: it does have one along the top. That is not a mistake in the drawing and it will not be a mistake in your wiring — it is how the CD4026 was designed in 1970, and it is what your display will show.

Before you decide that's ugly. Sketch a 6 without its top bar, then a 9 without its bottom bar. Now sketch b and q. The chip's choice makes 6 and 9 look like each other's mirror image, which is exactly what a 6 and a 9 are.

Section 3

One chip that counts and decodes

The CD4026 is two of the things you have already built, welded together.

In the Reaction Machine you used a CD4017: a counter with ten outputs, one for each count. That works for ten lamps in a row and is useless for a digit, because "count 7" is one wire and a 7 needs three bars lit at once.

The CD4026 has the same counter inside it — five flip-flops in a ring, which is the same trick — and then a wall of gates that turns the count into the seven answers. Its outputs are not "which count is it", they are a through g. Count and decode in one 16-pin package.

The three pins that count

Pin 1
CLOCK. Every time this goes from low to high, add one. At 9 it wraps to 0.
Pin 15
RESET. Hold it high and the count is 0. Let go and it counts from there.
Pin 2
CLOCK INHIBIT. High means ignore the clock. A freeze button.

The three pins that display

Pin 3
DISPLAY ENABLE IN. Low forces all seven outputs off. The count keeps running; you just can't see it.
Pin 5
CARRY OUT. One pulse per ten counts. This is the tens digit's clock.
Pin 14
UNGATED C. Segment c, ignoring pin 3. For divide-by-12 tricks. You will leave it empty.
Pin 3 is the one that wastes an afternoon. If DISPLAY ENABLE IN is left low — or left dangling, which on CMOS means "anything it feels like" — every segment output stays at 0 and your display is stone dead while the chip counts away happily inside. Pin 3 goes to + and stays there.

The clock input is a Schmitt trigger, and that is a present

The datasheet says something unusual about pin 1: clock rise and fall time, unlimited. Every other chip in this box has a limit. The CD4026 has none, because its clock input is a Schmitt trigger — the snap-decision input you met in the Siren Box, which refuses to dither near the threshold.

That means you can feed pin 1 a lazy, sagging, capacitor-shaped edge and it will still register exactly one count. Which is the whole reason Stage 3 works with one resistor and one capacitor instead of another chip.

Counter plus decoder in one part. In 1970 the reason to buy this chip was package count: one CD4026 replaced a counter chip, a decoder chip and the twenty-odd wires between them. That argument never went away. It is why a microcontroller today has counters, PWM and serial ports built onto the same silicon as the processor — not because it must, but because the wires between chips are the expensive part.
Section 4

The problem: your display is the wrong way round

This is the interesting part of the project, and it starts as bad news.

Seven-segment displays come in two kinds, and they are not interchangeable.

Common cathode

All seven LEDs have their − legs joined into one pin, which goes to the − rail. To light a bar you put + on it.

1 lights a segment.

Common anode

All seven LEDs have their + legs joined into one pin, which goes to the + rail. To light a bar you have to pull it down to −.

0 lights a segment.

The CD4026 puts out a 1 for every bar it wants lit. It was designed for common-cathode displays — the datasheet says so on the front page and then prints two suggested circuits, both with a common-cathode part in them.

The displays in this parts box are common anode.

So wired straight together, nothing works — and it fails in a confusing way. Every segment the chip wants off gets pulled to − and lights up. Every segment it wants on sits at 5 V with 5 V on the other side too, so no current flows and it stays dark. You get the photo-negative of the right answer: a 1 looks like a wonky 0 with a hole in it. Press Inverters in the simulator above to see it.

The fix is a chip full of NOT gates

Between every segment output and its bar, put an inverter. The chip says "light a", the inverter turns that 1 into a 0, and 0 is exactly what a common-anode display wants. Seven segments, seven inverters.

A CD4069 holds six inverters, so one digit needs one and a bit chips. That leftover "a bit" is annoying — until you notice the second digit needs one too. Both g segments come out of the same shared chip, so two digits cost three CD4069s rather than four.

Is there a lazier fix? Yes: buy common-cathode displays and skip all three chips. That is what an engineer with a budget does, and it's worth knowing that the right answer to a polarity mismatch is sometimes "order the other part". You are doing it the hard way because the hard way is the lesson, and because fourteen NOT gates cost you nothing today.
Polarity mismatch is a real job. "This chip drives the wrong way round" is one of the most common things a hardware engineer deals with, and inverting buffers exist as products precisely because of it. Notice also that the inverters do the work the CD4026 can't: a CD4026 output shoves out about 1 mA, which is a feeble segment. The CD4069 doing the sinking is what makes the digit bright enough to read.

Section 6

Rules for not breaking things

Same seven as always, plus one new one for the 16-pin chip.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every time. Not "mostly".
  2. The notch tells you which end is which Notch up, pin 1 is to its left, and the numbers run anticlockwise down the left side and back up the right.
  3. The CD4026 is a 16-pin chip: + on 16, − on 8 Not 14 and 7. Every other chip in this box is 14-pin, your hands will go to 14 and 7 out of habit, and 5 V into pin 14 of a CD4026 lands on the UNGATED C output. Check it twice.
  4. Never leave an input dangling Unused CD4069 inputs go to −. On the CD4026, pin 2 goes to − and pin 3 goes to +.
  5. Never wire an output straight to a rail That is a short circuit through the chip. The segment outputs go to inverter inputs, nowhere else.
  6. Every segment gets its own resistor Not one resistor in the common pin. See the box below for why.
  7. Warm chip means stop Pull the power and check rule 2 and rule 3.
Why not one resistor in the common pin? It would work, and it would save you thirteen resistors, and every digit would be a different brightness. A 1 lights two bars and gets the whole current between them; an 8 splits the same current eight ways and comes out faint. Counting from 7 to 8 would look like the battery dying. Per-segment resistors are how every real display is wired.
Section 7

The two pinouts, and one you have to find yourself

Chip pins come from the datasheet. Display pins come from a test.

The display's pins are not in a datasheet you own

Seven-segment displays are sold by the hundred by companies that never published a sheet for them. The ten-pin ones in kits are almost always wired the same way, and almost always is not good enough to build on. So test yours. It takes two minutes and it is a proper piece of engineering.

  1. Make a probe One wire from the + rail through a 1 kΩ resistor. The resistor is what stops you destroying a segment while you hunt. Another wire straight from the − rail.
  2. Find the common pin Hold the − wire on any pin and brush the probe across all the others. The pin that lights something no matter which other pin you touch is a common pin — and because these are common anode, you will find it the other way round: put the probe on the candidate and the − wire on the others. Ten-pin displays have two commons, wired together inside; pins 3 and 8.
  3. Name the segments Leave the probe on the common. Touch − to each remaining pin in turn and write down which bar lights. That is your segment map, and it is the one the rest of this build uses.
  4. Fill in the table There's one in the worksheet. Write it in pencil before you wire a single segment.
Never touch the probe straight to a segment pin with no resistor. A bare LED across 5 V draws whatever it can and dies in about a second, usually without a flash to tell you.
This is the normal state of affairs. Professionals meet undocumented parts constantly — a display from a scrapped machine, a sensor from a supplier who went under, a connector on a board nobody has the drawing for. The method is always the same: a current-limited probe, one pin at a time, and a written table at the end. The written table is the part beginners skip and regret.
Stage 1The display alone ~25 min

Spell a digit with your hands

No chips yet. One display, seven resistors, and a wire you move around.

Before anything drives the display, you should be able to drive it. By the end of this stage you will have made a 7 with three wires, which tells you your segment map is right — and finding that out now is much cheaper than finding it out through five chips.

Now take one wire from the − rail and touch the free end of each resistor in turn. One bar lights per resistor. Then:

  • Touch a, b and c at once — three wires, or one wire bridging three rows. That's a 7.
  • Add d, e and f for a 0. Add g as well for an 8.
  • Look at how bright one bar is. That brightness is what the finished machine will have, because from here on the inverters pull the segment down to − exactly like your wire does.
Expect about 4 mA a segment. Clearly lit indoors, not sunlight-bright. If a bar is much dimmer than its neighbours, you have a 1 kΩ in there by mistake — the colour bands for 220 Ω are red-red-brown and for 1 kΩ brown-black-red.
Build the output first. Given a chain of five unfamiliar things, start at the end you can test alone. Every segment you prove here is a segment you will not suspect in Stage 2, and in Stage 2 you will have fourteen new wires to suspect instead.

Seven segments, seven inverters, two chips

This is the long table. Work down it one row at a time and tick each one. Every row is the same shape: counter output → inverter in, then inverter out → resistor → display.

  • Six of CD4069 #1's inputs used, none spare — that chip is full.
  • One of CD4069 #2's inputs used. Tie its other five inputs (pins 3, 5, 9, 11, 13) to −. Floating CMOS inputs make a chip warm and make the board superstitious.

Now the crudest possible clock

Put a 100 kΩ resistor from CD4026 pin 1 to the − rail, so the clock input is held low. Then take a loose wire from the + rail and tap pin 1 with it.

The digit changes. Power up and the display shows something — probably not 0, because a CD4026 wakes up on whatever count it feels like. Tap pin 1 a few times and it climbs: 3, 4, 5… 9, 0, 1.

Now count carefully, and watch it cheat. Tap once. Did it go up by one? Tap again. Sooner or later one tap jumps you three or four digits. You did not slip. Metal contacts bounce — the wire touches, springs off, touches again, several times in the first few thousandths of a second, and each bounce is a perfectly good clock edge as far as the chip is concerned. Stage 3 is entirely about this.
Decoding, seen from the outside. Cover the display and look at the seven wires going into the inverters. There is no "7" anywhere on that bundle — just seven yes-or-no answers that happen to look like a 7 when arranged in the right shape. Every screen you have ever looked at is doing a version of this.
Stage 3One press, one count ~20 min

Beat the bounce with a resistor and a capacitor

Two components, and the most useful five minutes of theory in the box.

A push button is two bits of springy metal. When they meet they bounce apart and back several times over a few thousandths of a second. Your finger feels one press; the chip sees a burst of them. Fixing this is called debouncing, and there are two ways: a chip that waits, or a capacitor that refuses to be hurried. The CD4026's Schmitt-trigger clock input means we can use the cheap one.

FromToWhy
Remove the 100 kΩ from pin 1 to − It is about to go the other way up
100 kΩCD4026 pin 1 → + railHolds the clock high, and charges the capacitor slowly
0.1 µF ("104")CD4026 pin 1 → − railThe component that will not be hurried
ButtonCD4026 pin 1 → − railPress to pull the clock down

Press the button. Nothing happens. Let go. The count goes up by one, exactly one, every time.

Why it works, in four steps

  1. Resting, the capacitor is full The 100 kΩ has charged it to 5 V. Pin 1 sees a clean high.
  2. Press: the capacitor dumps The button is a near-zero-resistance path to −, so the capacitor empties instantly and pin 1 goes low. Low is not a counting edge — the CD4026 counts on the way up — so nothing happens yet.
  3. Bounce: the capacitor ignores it Each time the contacts spring apart, the only thing refilling the capacitor is that 100 kΩ, which takes about 10 milliseconds to do it. A bounce lasts a fraction of a millisecond, so the voltage creeps up a few hundredths of a volt and gets slammed back down. It never gets near the threshold. No edge.
  4. Release: one slow, clean climb Contacts open for good, the capacitor charges over about 10 ms, and pin 1 rises smoothly past the threshold once. One count. The Schmitt trigger is what makes that sluggish ramp legal.
Your button now counts when you let go. That feels wrong for about a minute and then feels completely normal — and it is what the buttons on a microwave do. Hold one down and watch: nothing happens until you release.

Worth trying, so the numbers stop being abstract:

  • Pull the capacitor out. The bouncing comes straight back. Put it in. It goes. That one part is the whole fix.
  • Swap the 100 kΩ for a 10 kΩ. Now the capacitor refills in 1 ms, which is about as long as a bounce lasts — so some presses count twice again. The size is the design, not just the presence.
  • Swap it for 1 MΩ. Now it takes a tenth of a second to recover, and pressing fast loses counts. Too slow is also broken.
Every button you have ever pressed is debounced. Keyboards do it in firmware — read the key, then ignore that key for 10 ms. Your circuit does it with one capacitor. The number in both cases is the same 10 ms, because it comes from the springiness of metal, not from electronics.
Stage 4Reset, hold, blank ~15 min

Three control pins, three buttons

Short stage. Each of these is one wire and one button, and each one turns the thing into a slightly different machine.

RESET — back to zero

FromToWhy
100 kΩCD4026 pin 15 → − railHolds RESET low, replacing the plain wire from Stage 2

No capacitor on this one, and that is a decision, not laziness. RESET bounces just as badly as the count button. It does not matter: bouncing a reset means resetting to zero five times instead of once, and zero five times is still zero. An operation you can repeat harmlessly is called idempotent, and spotting one is how you know a part needs no protection.

Two different kinds of "off". HOLD stops the counting. BLANK stops the showing. Mixing those two up is a real bug in real products — the pause button that keeps the timer running, the muted alarm still marching towards its deadline. Your board lets you feel the difference with two fingers.
Stage 5The tens digit ~45 min

Past nine

The same thing twice, plus one wire that is the whole idea of carrying.

Stop here and you have a working scoreboard that counts to nine, which is enough for a quiz. Carry on and you get 99, and the part of arithmetic everyone learns at seven years old turns out to be one pin.

The second digit is a copy: another CD4026, a third CD4069 for its segments a–f, and the spare inverter on CD4069 #2 for its g. Wire it exactly as Stage 2, then:

Count up to 9 and press once more. The units digit rolls to 0 and the tens digit becomes 1. Ten.

Why the carry lands at the right moment

CARRY OUT is not a short pulse. It is high while the count is 0 to 4 and low while it is 5 to 9 — a square wave at one tenth of the clock. Which means its rising edge happens at exactly one place in the cycle: the instant the count goes from 9 back to 0.

And the tens digit counts on rising edges. So it ticks once, at nine-to-zero, which is what carrying means.

Nearly a famous bug. If the CD4026's carry had been built the other way up, its rising edge would land at 4→5 and your scoreboard would count 03, 04, 15, 16 — the tens digit ticking over halfway through the units. Whoever designed this chip in 1970 chose the polarity so that the obvious wiring is also the correct wiring. You can check their work on page 3 of the datasheet.

Where it stops

At 99, one more press gives 00. There is no hundreds digit and nothing warns you; the number simply wraps, exactly the way the 9 wrapped in Stage 2. Two digits hold a hundred different numbers and not one more.

This is overflow, and it is not a toy problem. Add a third CD4026 and you get to 999 — the chain works to any length, one chip per digit, which is precisely why CARRY OUT exists. But however many you add there is a last one, and the number after its 9 is 0. Sixteen-bit games wrapping a score at 65,535, a spreadsheet losing pennies past a certain size, the Y2K panic: all the same pin, further along.
BonusFour upgradesas long as you like

If it is going to live on the shelf

  1. A clock instead of a finger The Siren Box's CD40106 oscillator, slowed down to one pulse a second with a 1 MΩ and a 10 µF, wired to pin 1 instead of the button. You now have a seconds counter — and a stopwatch, if you put HOLD on a button and RESET on another.
  2. Wire it to the Quiz Buzzer Player 1's lamp output can drive the count input, so the scoreboard adds a point every time they buzz in. Two projects become one machine.
  3. Count down instead of up You cannot: the CD4026 only goes one way. Look up what chip you would need (the word to search for is "up/down counter") and notice that this is how engineering usually goes — not "how do I make this part do it", but "which part already does it".
  4. Blank the leading zero "07" looks like a machine; "7" looks like a number. The chip that does this automatically is the CD4033, the CD4026's twin — same pinout, but pins 3 and 4 do ripple blanking instead of display enable. It is in the same datasheet, one column to the right.
Section 8

When it doesn't work

Display projects fail in their own particular ways. These are them.

What you seeAlmost always
Display completely dead, but the chip is coolPin 3 is not at +. This is the most common fault in this project by a distance.
The digits look like photo-negativesA segment is wired from the CD4026 straight to the display, skipping its inverter. Count the segments that are wrong — that is how many wires to re-check.
One bar never lightsThree suspects in a row: the resistor, the inverter's output pin, the segment pin. Test the segment with your Stage 1 probe first — it rules out two of the three.
One bar never goes offIts inverter input is floating. Check the wire from the CD4026's segment pin.
Jumps two or three per pressBounce. Capacitor missing, in the wrong row, or you used a 22 pF instead of a 104.
Counts down, or randomlyThe 100 kΩ is still going to − instead of +. Stage 3 turns it round.
Never leaves 0Pin 15 is high. Your RESET button is stuck, or the 100 kΩ pull-down is missing.
Stuck on one number, button does nothingPin 2 is high. HOLD is on.
Tens digit ticks at the wrong timeCarry is wired to the tens chip's pin 2 instead of pin 1.
A chip gets warmPower in backwards — and on the CD4026 that means + on 14 instead of 16. Unplug now.
Everything is wrong at onceUnplug, walk away five minutes, re-read one table. This is what real engineers do.
The technique that finds segment faults fastest. Hold RESET down so the display shows 0, which lights six of the seven bars and leaves g dark. One glance tells you about six segments. Then count to 8: all seven on. Two numbers test the whole display.
