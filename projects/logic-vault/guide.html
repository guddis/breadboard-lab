Logic gates · Project 01

The Logic
Vault

A combination safe with no computer inside it. You hide a secret code in four wires; someone else has to find it. Everything that decides right or wrong is done by three chips that only know how to compare, and one that knows how to say no.

Section 1

What you're building

Play with the finished thing before you build any of it.

Four dials on the front. Four secret wires hidden round the back. The vault compares them, one pair at a time. If all four pairs agree, the green light comes on and the vault lights up. Press OPEN with even one dial wrong and a buzzer goes off.

Click any dial to flip it between 1 and 0. This is a simulation of the exact circuit you're about to wire — same chips, same pins, same behaviour.

The whole idea in one line. A gate called XNOR answers the question "are these two wires the same?" Use four of them, then ask "did all four say yes?" That's a combination lock.
Section 2

One wire, two words

Everything below rests on this, so it's worth thirty seconds.

A wire in this project is only ever doing one of two things. It's either connected to the + rail, or connected to the − rail. That's it. There's no in-between, no "a bit on".

Connected to +

About 5 volts. Engineers write this as 1 and say "high". You'll also hear true, or set.

Connected to −

Zero volts. Written 0, said "low". Also false, or clear. Note this is not the same as unplugged.

Unplugged is a third thing, and it's trouble. A chip input with nothing on it doesn't read 0 — it drifts, picks up radio and mains hum from the room, and flickers. These CMOS chips are especially twitchy about it. Every input in this project goes to + or to − or to another chip's output. Never nowhere.

A logic gate is a lump of circuitry with one or two input wires and one output wire. It looks at its inputs, applies one fixed rule, and drives its output to 1 or 0. It does this constantly, with no clock and no program — change an input and the output follows within a few billionths of a second.

The rule is written as a truth table: every possible combination of inputs, and what the output does for each. Two inputs means four rows. That's the entire specification of a gate — learn the table and you know the part.

Section 3

The five gates you need

One chip each. You'll test all five by hand in Stages 1–3.

Each chip below holds several copies of the same gate. A CD4081 is four separate AND gates sharing one lump of plastic — they don't interact, you just get four for the price of one.

Why these five? AND, OR and NOT are the classic three — every digital circuit ever built can be made from them alone. XOR and XNOR are shortcuts for a job that comes up constantly: comparing. You could build XNOR out of the other three, and in Challenge C you will. But there's a chip for it, so we use the chip.
Section 4

The trick: a gate that asks a question

This is the idea the whole vault is built on.

Look hard at the XNOR table. Ignore the name for a moment and just read the pattern:

ABOut In words
001 same → yes
010 different → no
100 different → no
111 same → yes

An XNOR gate outputs 1 when its two inputs match, whatever they happen to be. It doesn't care what the values are, only whether they agree. It's a machine that answers one question: are these two the same?

So: put a dial on one input and a hidden secret wire on the other. The output is now a light that means "this dial is correct". Do that four times and you have four correctness lights. Then all you need is a gate that asks "are all four of those lights on?", and you have a combination lock.

This is a real part. Engineers call it a comparator, and the datasheet for the CD4077 lists "logical comparators" as its first application. Every time a computer checks a password, compares two numbers, or spots a corrupted file, something shaped like this is doing the work — just with 64 of them side by side instead of 4.
Section 5

How the vault thinks

Four signals in, two decisions out, three chips in between.

Scroll back to the simulator and watch the wires light up as you flip dials. Here's the same journey in words:

  1. Compare, four times over Each dial meets its secret wire at an XNOR gate inside the CD4077. Four gates, four answers. Each output is 1 for "this one's right".
  2. Judge Those four answers go into one 4-input NAND gate inside the CD4012. A NAND outputs 0 only when every input is 1 — so its output is 0 when the code is fully correct, and 1 the moment anything is wrong. That signal is LOCKED.
  3. Flip it LOCKED is backwards for driving a green light, so we invert it. The CD4012 has a second 4-input NAND doing nothing, so we use that — see below. Its output is UNLOCKED.
  4. Act LOCKED lights the red LED. UNLOCKED lights the green LED and the vault lights. And the CD4081 combines LOCKED with the OPEN button to fire the buzzer: alarm only when you try the door and the code is wrong.

The free inverter

We need a NOT gate for exactly one signal, and putting a whole sixth chip on the board for one gate would be silly. There's a trick instead.

A NAND gate outputs 0 only when all its inputs are 1. So tie all four inputs of the spare gate to the same wire. Now there are only two cases: all four are 0, or all four are 1 — and the gate outputs 1, then 0. That is precisely a NOT gate. A NAND with its inputs tied together becomes an inverter.

Why this matters more than it looks. NAND can imitate every other gate — NOT, AND, OR, all of them. It's functionally complete, and chip factories exploit this: it's often cheaper to build a whole processor from one repeated NAND cell than to make five different kinds of gate. You just used the same trick a chip designer would.

Why the buzzer needs a transistor

These chips are from 1968 and they are polite. A CD4000 output at 5 V can push about 3 milliamps before its voltage starts sagging. An LED will glow from that — dimly. A buzzer wants ten times more and simply won't sound.

So each output that has real work to do gets a PN2222 transistor: the chip pushes a tiny current into the transistor's base, and the transistor lets a much bigger current through from the + rail. A small signal controlling a large one — that's an amplifier, and it's the single most useful thing a transistor does.

Chips think, transistors shove. Keep the two jobs separate in your head and a lot of electronics stops being confusing. If something needs to be bright, loud, or move, a logic chip won't do it alone.

Section 7

Seven rules for not breaking things

Read once now. Re-read rule 2 every time you place a chip.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every single time. Rewiring a live board is how chips die and how you lose an afternoon to a fault you created yourself.
  2. The notch tells you which way round the chip goes Every chip has a notch or dot at one end. Notch to the left, and pin 1 is the bottom-left. Backwards means pin 14 gets ground and pin 7 gets +5 V — the chip heats up and dies in seconds.
  3. Never leave an input dangling Including inputs on gates you aren't using. Tie every unused input to −.
  4. Never wire an output straight to + or − That's a short circuit through the chip's own transistors.
  5. The electrolytic capacitor has a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, it can pop.
  6. Touch something metal before handling chips Static from a carpet is thousands of volts. CMOS hates it.
  7. Warm chip means stop Pull the power immediately and check rule 2, then rule 4.
Section 8

Reading a chip, and reading a breadboard

Two skills. Ten minutes. Then the wiring is just following a list.

Pin numbering

How a breadboard is joined up

Some cheap boards split the rails in the middle. If the left half of your board works and the right half is dead, that's why. Bridge the two halves with a jumper and carry on.

The pinouts you'll be using

Four of these six chips share an identical pin layout — inputs on 1 + 2, 5 + 6, 8 + 9, 12 + 13, with outputs on 3, 4, 10 and 11. Learn it once and you've learned four chips.

Build a test bench

One chip, two dials, one light. Everything later is this, repeated.

Now power up. Take the two loose ends of DIAL A and DIAL B and plug each into either the + rail or the − rail. Four combinations. Try all four, and write the result in worksheet table 2 as you go.

The light will be dim, and that's correct. Remember the 3 milliamp limit — the chip is doing its best. Stage 5 adds transistors and you'll get proper brightness then. If it's completely dark, the LED is in backwards: long leg toward +.
Why the little capacitor? When a gate switches, it gulps current for a few billionths of a second. That gulp makes the supply voltage dip, and the dip can make other gates twitch. The 0.1 µF sits right next to the chip as a tiny local reservoir. Real circuit boards have one per chip, always. It's the most-skipped and most-worth-it component in electronics.
Stage 2Meet the family ~30 min

Swap the chip, keep the bench

Three more gates, no rewiring. This is the payoff for four chips sharing a pinout.

Power down. Lift the CD4081 out and drop in a CD4071. Every wire stays exactly where it is — same power pins, same inputs on 1 and 2, same output on 3. Power up and run the four combinations again.

Then do it again with the CD4070. Fill in worksheet tables 3 and 4.

Then the odd one out

The CD4069 is six NOT gates, and NOT has only one input, so it needs a different layout: input on pin 1, output on pin 2. Move your output wire from pin 3 to pin 2, pull the DIAL B jumper out entirely, and leave DIAL A on pin 1. Two combinations only.

Power down before every chip swap. Pulling a live chip is the fastest way to kill one. Get in the habit now, while nothing you've built matters much.

By the end of this stage you've personally verified four of the five gates by hand. Compare your XOR table with the others — it's the first one that isn't obvious, and it's the one that leads somewhere.

Stage 3The comparator ~15 min

Find the gate that answers questions

One more swap, and the vault becomes obvious.

Same bench. Drop in the CD4077 — inputs 1 and 2, output 3, exactly as before. Run all four combinations and fill in worksheet table 5.

Now stop and compare it against your XOR table from Stage 2. Every single row is flipped. XOR says "these are different"; XNOR says "these are the same". They're the same gate with an inverter bolted on the end, which is exactly what the N in the name means.

Then try this. Leave DIAL B in the + rail and don't touch it again. It's now a fixed secret: a 1. Flip only DIAL A.

SecretYour dial LightMeans
10off wrong
11on right

That is a working one-digit combination lock. Move DIAL B to − and the secret becomes 0 — the lock still works, it just wants the opposite answer. The secret is the wire. Nothing was programmed; you changed the machine.

You're now four repetitions away from the finished vault. Everything after this is scale and plumbing.
Stage 4Four at once ~30 min

All four comparators

Use the whole CD4077. Four dials, four secrets, four lights.

Keep the CD4077 in place. It has four independent XNOR gates and you've been using one. Wire up the other three the same way, plus a yellow LED on each output:

Each of the four outputs gets its own 220 Ω resistor into a yellow LED, short leg to the − rail. Four lights, one per digit.

  • Set the four secret jumpers to a code — say 1 0 1 1 — and leave them.
  • Set all four dials to 0. One light should be on: digit 2, the one whose secret is also 0.
  • Work along the dials until all four lights are on. You've cracked it.
Notice how easy that was. Each light told you about exactly one dial, so you never had to guess a whole code — four flips and you're in. Hold onto that thought; it comes back in Stage 7 and it turns out to be a real security problem with a real name.
What you've built has a name. This is a 4-bit comparator. Widen it to 64 bits and give it a subtraction unit and you've got the part of a processor that decides whether one number equals another — the thing behind every if statement in every program ever written.

The output block

Two transistors, wired the same way. On each PN2222, flat side toward you and legs down: left = emitter, middle = base, right = collector.

Q1 — the red LOCKED light

  • CD4012 pin 1 → 10 kΩ → Q1 base
  • Q1 emitter → − rail
  • + rail → 220 Ω → red LED long leg
  • Red LED short leg → Q1 collector

Q2 — green, plus the vault lights

  • CD4012 pin 13 → 5.1 kΩ → Q2 base
  • Q2 emitter → − rail
  • + rail → 220 Ω → green LED long leg
  • + rail → 220 Ω → white LED long leg (×2)
  • All three short legs → Q2 collector

Power up. Red should be lit. Set the dials to the secret code and red goes out as green and both whites come on — brightly this time.

Why 10 kΩ on Q1 but 5.1 kΩ on Q2? The LOCKED wire is busy — besides Q1 it also feeds five chip inputs. A smaller resistor would draw more current out of that pin and drag its voltage down until the chips stopped reading it as a clean 1. Pin 13 drives nothing but Q2, so it can afford to be generous. Sharing one output between several jobs is called fan-out, and every real design has a budget for it.
Stage 6The alarm ~25 min

Make it shout

The button, the third transistor, and the buzzer.

A buzzer that screams whenever the code is wrong would scream almost constantly. So the alarm needs two conditions at once: the code is wrong AND someone is trying the door. That's a job for an AND gate.

The OPEN button

Place the button so it straddles the channel. A 4-legged tactile button has its legs joined in pairs, and using two diagonally opposite legs is the choice that's always right.

That 10 kΩ is a pull-down, and it's rule 3 in action. With the button un-pressed, its leg is connected to nothing — a floating input. The resistor gently holds the node at 0 until the button slams it up to +. Leave it out and your alarm will fire at random. It's in the troubleshooting table for a reason.

Stage 7Play as long as you like

Now break it

Set a code, hand it to someone, and think about what you've made.

  1. Hide the secret Set the four secret jumpers without anyone watching, then tape a folded card over that end of the board. Real safes have a back panel for the same reason.
  2. Hand it over All they get is four dials and the OPEN button. Time them.
  3. Do the counting Four dials, two positions each: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 possible codes. Worst case is 16 tries, average about 8. Work through worksheet section 8.
  4. Now put the yellow lights back Wire the four match LEDs onto the CD4077 outputs again and hand it over a second time. Watch how fast it falls: four flips, no guessing.
You've just discovered a side channel. The lock was never broken — 16 codes is still 16 codes. But the machine leaked, telling the attacker about each digit separately, and that turned one hard problem into four trivial ones. Engineers call anything a machine gives away without meaning to a side channel: a light, a sound, how long something takes, how warm it gets, how much power it draws. Real bank cards have been broken exactly this way — not by beating the maths, but by watching the power supply flicker while the card thought. It's also why a good login screen says "wrong username or password" and never which one was wrong.

Make it harder

  • Add dials 5 and 6 using a second CD4077 — 64 codes.
  • Feed the spare CD4081 gates so the alarm latches on and needs a reset.
  • Put the green LED on a switch you can hide, so cracking it is silent.
BonusIf you want more

Two upgrades

Make it remember — CD4013

Right now the green light goes out the moment a dial moves. A real safe stays open until you close it. That needs memory, and the CD4013 is two flip-flops — the smallest memory there is, one bit each.

Feed UNLOCKED into the SET pin (pin 6) and a push button into RESET (pin 4), then take your green light from Q (pin 1) instead of the CD4012. Now the vault latches open when the code is first correct and stays open until you press reset. Pin 14 is +, pin 7 is −, as always; tie DATA (pin 5) and CLOCK (pin 3) to −, since set and reset work on their own.

This is the line between two kinds of circuit. Everything before this was combinational — outputs depend only on inputs right now. A flip-flop makes it sequential: the output depends on what happened earlier. That single step is the difference between a calculator and a computer.

Count the failed attempts — CD4026

The CD4026 is a counter that drives a 7-segment display directly. Feed the ALARM signal from CD4081 pin 3 into its CLOCK (pin 1) and wire its seven outputs through 220 Ω resistors to a display's segments. Every failed attempt ticks the number up. Three strikes and you're caught.

Your displays are common anode, so the common pin goes to + and the CD4026 is designed for common cathode — you'll need to invert the segment drives with a CD4069, or use the display upside-down in your head. This one is a genuine puzzle; that's the point.

Section 9

When it doesn't work

It will happen. Every one of these has a boring cause.

What you seeAlmost always
Nothing lights at allPower isn't reaching pin 14 and pin 7. Check both, on every chip.
A chip gets warmPower in backwards. Unplug now. Notch left, pin 14 top-left.
Lights flicker or change on their ownA floating input. Find the input with nothing on it and tie it to −.
Green never comes onA MATCH wire is on the wrong CD4012 pin. The inputs are 2, 3, 4, 5 — never 1.
Green is always onThe four jumpers from pin 1 to pins 9–12 are missing or misplaced.
Both red and green on togetherOne of those four jumpers is on pin 8 — that's a no-connect pin.
LEDs very dim in stages 1–4Normal. 3 mA is all the chip has. Stage 5 fixes it.
An LED never lightsBackwards. Long leg toward +.
Buzzer silent but the logic is rightBuzzer polarity, or it's the passive buzzer rather than the active one.
Buzzer sounds at randomThe button's 10 kΩ pull-down is missing.
Everything is wrong at onceUnplug, walk away for five minutes, come back and re-read one table. This is what real engineers do.
The one technique worth learning. Don't stare at the whole board. Pick one signal and follow it: is MATCH 1 doing the right thing? Put an LED on it and check. Then MATCH 2. Halve the problem, then halve it again. Four checks find a fault anywhere in sixteen wires.
