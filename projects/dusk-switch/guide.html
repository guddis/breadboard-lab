Sensors and thresholds · Project 08

The Dusk
Switch

Every project in this box so far has been told what to do by a switch — a thing that is 1 or 0 because your finger says so. A light sensor does not do that. It hands you 2.3 volts and walks away. Somebody has to decide where 1 stops and 0 begins, and this is the project where you find out how badly that goes if you decide it carelessly.

Section 1

What you're building

A light that comes on when it gets dark. Drag the slider and watch the trace — the interesting part is what happens as it crosses the line.

Press hover at dusk. It creeps the light up and down across the level where this gate has to decide — which is exactly what Stage 2 will ask you to do with your hand. With the plain CD4011 fitted, the lamp cannot make up its mind: watch the counter climb. Now press Gate to fit the CD4093 and watch it stop.

Light

The teal trace is the sensor's voltage. The orange band is the gate's region of indecision. The lower orange trace is the lamp. Count its edges — that number is the whole project.

The whole idea in one line. A sensor gives you a smooth, noisy, continuous voltage. A logic gate needs a yes or a no. The circuit that converts one into the other honestly is called a Schmitt trigger, and it works by refusing to change its mind twice in a row.
Section 2

A photoresistor is a resistor that listens

No chip, no magic, no pins to get wrong. Two legs, either way round.

The little round part with the squiggle on its face is a photoresistor, or LDR — light-dependent resistor. It is made of cadmium sulphide, and light knocks electrons loose inside it, which makes it easier for current to get through. Bright light means low resistance. Darkness means high resistance. That is the entire datasheet.

Those numbers are somebody else's sensor. Photoresistors vary enormously between parts, and nobody ships a datasheet with the ones in a kit. Treat the table as the right shape and measure your own in Stage 1 — which you can do without a multimeter, with a trick worth knowing.

A resistance is not a voltage, so we make one

A gate cannot read ohms. It reads volts. So put the sensor in series with an ordinary fixed resistor between + and −, and read the voltage where they meet. That arrangement is a voltage divider, and it is the most-used circuit in electronics.

The useful thing about a divider is that the answer is a ratio, which makes one number worth remembering:

The middle sits at half the supply when the two resistances are equal. So if your fixed resistor is 10 kΩ, the midpoint of the divider arrives exactly when the sensor also reads 10 kΩ. Choosing the fixed resistor is choosing how dark it has to get. That one sentence is the whole design of this project.
Dividers are everywhere, and this is why. Every sensor that changes its resistance — a thermistor, a strain gauge in a bathroom scale, a fuel-tank float, a moisture probe — is read by putting it in a divider and looking at the middle. The rest of the machine only ever sees a voltage.
Section 3

Where does 1 stop and 0 begin?

Every gate has an answer to this. Its answer is the source of the trouble.

Feed 5 V into a CD4011 and it reads 1. Feed it 0 V and it reads 0. Feed it 2.5 V — exactly halfway — and it has to pick one, and whichever it picks it is only just picking it. A hair either side flips the answer.

That is fine when the input comes from a switch, because a switch is never halfway. It is not fine when the input comes from the sky slowly getting dark.

The problem is that nothing is smooth

Your sensor's voltage is not a clean line. Superimposed on it are:

  • Mains flicker. Room lighting brightens and dims 100 times a second, and the LDR follows it.
  • Your hand. A shadow edge is never still.
  • The supply. Batteries sag, wires pick things up, and the other chips on your board are switching.

Add them up and the sensor voltage wobbles by a few tens of millivolts. Which does not matter at all — until the voltage happens to be sitting on the gate's threshold. Then every wobble is a decision, and the lamp flickers.

This is not a fault, and no amount of careful wiring fixes it. A single threshold and a noisy signal will always chatter at the crossing. The fix is not cleaner wiring; it is a better question.
Chattering costs real money. A thermostat that chatters switches the boiler on and off a hundred times an hour and wears out the relay. A lift-door sensor that chatters reopens the door. A car's reversing sensor that chatters beeps nonsense. Every one of those is this exact circuit, and every one is fixed the same way.
Section 4

Two thresholds instead of one

The idea is almost insultingly simple, and it has a long name.

Stop asking "is the voltage above the line?". Ask two questions instead:

  1. If the lamp is off — turn it on only when the voltage falls below the lower line.
  2. If the lamp is on — turn it off only when the voltage climbs above the upper line.

Now a wobble cannot flip anything, because after each decision the line it would have to cross to change its mind has moved away from it. The gap between the two lines is called hysteresis, from the Greek for "lagging behind".

The CD4093 has it built in

A CD4093 is a quad 2-input NAND with Schmitt-trigger inputs, and the front page of its datasheet says the useful things out loud: "Schmitt-trigger action on each input with no external components", "hysteresis voltage typically 0.9 V at VDD = 5 V", and "no limit on input rise and fall times" — which is exactly the promise you need when the input is the sunset.

Its pinout is identical to the CD4011's. Same 14 pins, same gates in the same places. That is what makes Stage 3 a fifteen-minute stage: you pull one chip out, put the other in, and change nothing else.

Read the guaranteed column, not the typical one. Typical is 0.9 V. The datasheet only promises 0.3 V, and permits as much as 1.6 V. So the chip you have in your hand has somewhere between a third of a volt and a volt and a half of hysteresis, you do not get to know which, and you cannot change it. Stage 4 is about what to do when you need to know.
Hysteresis is a design decision, not a defect. A house thermostat heats to 21 °C and does not switch on again until 19.5 °C. That 1.5-degree gap is deliberate, it is the same idea as this, and it is why your boiler does not click on and off all evening.

Worth knowing before you leave it running in a shed.
Section 6

Rules for not breaking things

Shorter than usual. One chip at a time, and only one new hazard.

  1. Unplug the power before you swap the chip You will swap between the CD4011 and the CD4093 more than once. A live swap is the one that kills a chip.
  2. Both chips are + on 14 and − on 7 And they are the same pinout as each other, which is the point.
  3. Never leave an input dangling Unused inputs go to −. On a NAND that holds the gate's output at 1 and keeps it quiet.
  4. A NAND becomes a NOT when you tie its two inputs together That is not a bodge, it is the Gate Bench's trick, and this whole project uses it.
  5. Never wire an output to + or − Outputs drive gate inputs, LEDs through a resistor, or a transistor base through a resistor.
  6. The transistor's legs are not interchangeable PN2222, flat side toward you, legs down: E – B – C. Backwards, it does nothing and gets warm.
  7. Warm chip means stop Pull the power and check rules 2 and 5.
The one habit for this project. Change one resistor at a time and write down what happened before you change the next. Half of this build is measurement, and a measurement you did not write down did not happen.
Section 7

The pinouts

One drawing does both chips. Then the transistor, which has three legs and no notch.

Why the sensor needs no pinout. It is a resistor. It has no polarity, no power pins and no orientation, and you can put it in either way round. Enjoy it; the thermistor in the bonus is the same.
Stage 1The divider, and your own sensor ~25 min

Measure a resistance without a meter

No chips yet. One sensor, one resistor, one LED, and a genuinely clever trick.

You need to know what your photoresistor reads at the light level where you want the lamp to switch. You probably have no multimeter. You do not need one, because the divider tells you: when the two resistances are equal, the middle sits at half the supply. So if you can spot "half the supply", you can spot "the sensor now equals my fixed resistor".

And spotting half the supply is exactly what a logic gate does for a living. But before the chip goes in, do the crude version:

FromToWhy
Supply +Top red railPower in
Supply −Top blue railGround in
+ railOne leg of the photoresistorSensor on top
Other leg of the photoresistora spare row — call it S S is the voltage you are making
Row S10 kΩ → − railThe fixed half. This is the resistor you will swap
Row S1 kΩ → LED long leg, short leg to − A rough brightness meter

Power up and play with the light. Cover the sensor and the LED dims; shine a torch and it brightens. You are watching a voltage, not a light — the LED is reporting on S.

  • Cover the sensor completely. The LED should go nearly out.
  • Swap the 10 kΩ for 1 MΩ. Now the LED is bright even in a dim room, because the sensor is the smaller resistance almost all the time.
  • Swap it for 1 kΩ. Now the LED is dim unless you point a torch at it.
  • Put the 10 kΩ back.
You have just learned the most useful thing in this project. The fixed resistor is not a detail. It sets which light level counts as dark. Big fixed resistor means the circuit thinks it is bright nearly always; small means it thinks it is dark nearly always. The one you want is the one that is comparable to your sensor at the moment you care about.
This is a null measurement, and it is how precision used to be done. You are not measuring the sensor's resistance; you are finding the resistor that balances it and reading the answer off the resistor, which you already know the value of. Chemists weighed things this way for two centuries, and a Wheatstone bridge — still in every strain gauge today — is the same idea with four resistors.
Stage 2One gate, and a disappointment ~25 min

It works. Then watch it not work.

The CD4011 goes in, the lamp obeys the dark, and then you look closely.

One gate is all this needs. Tie a NAND's two inputs together and it becomes a NOT: high in, low out. Feed it S and its output is the opposite of the light — which is exactly what a nightlight wants.

Power up. Room light: the LED is off. Cup your hand over the sensor: the LED comes on. Let go: it goes off. That is a working dusk switch, built out of one gate, and it took four wires.

The LED is dim, and that is the chip, not your wiring. A CD4011 output pushes about 1 mA. It is enough to see indoors and nowhere near enough to light a room, which is what Stage 5 is for.

Now do it slowly

Move your hand towards the sensor as slowly as you can bear, so the light creeps down instead of dropping. Watch the LED at the moment it changes.

It stutters. Two, five, a dozen flickers in the half-second while your hand is at the critical distance, and then it settles. Do it again and you get a different number of flickers.

  • Try it with the room light on and then off. Mains-powered room light flickers 100 times a second and makes it noticeably worse.
  • Hold your hand still at exactly the distance where it cannot decide. It will sit there buzzing between on and off indefinitely.
  • Write down roughly how many flickers you get, in the worksheet. You will compare it in fifteen minutes.
Do not try to fix this by rewiring. There is nothing wrong with your board. The circuit is doing precisely what you asked: comparing a wobbling voltage against one fixed line, and reporting every single crossing. You asked a bad question.
This is the bug that teaches engineers to distrust "it works". It worked when you tested it quickly, in one light level, moving your hand at a normal speed. It fails only in the slow, marginal, realistic case — dusk. Products ship with this bug constantly, and the reason is that testing the easy case feels like testing.
Stage 3One chip, no rewiring ~15 min

The fifteen-minute stage

Pull out the CD4011. Put in the CD4093. Touch nothing else.

Power off. Lift the CD4011 out — rock it gently from both ends rather than levering one end, or you will bend the legs. Put the CD4093 in, notch the same way round. Power on.

Do the slow-hand test again.

It snaps. One clean transition, every time, at any speed you like, including holding your hand at the worst possible distance and waiting. The flicker is not reduced; it is gone.

  • Hold your hand at the distance that made the CD4011 buzz. Nothing. It has already decided.
  • Notice the lamp now turns on slightly later than it used to, and turns off slightly earlier. That is the two thresholds being in different places.
  • Find the two positions of your hand — one where it switches on, one where it switches off — and notice they are not the same position. Mark them, and measure the gap with a ruler for the worksheet.
Nothing was added. No capacitor, no extra resistor, no second chip, no software. The difference lives inside the chip's input, in a few extra transistors that move the threshold each time the output changes. One part number, one order code, and the fault is designed out rather than patched over.
Choosing the right part is engineering, and it is the cheap kind. The CD4093 costs the same as the CD4011. A whole class of bug disappears for free because somebody in 1975 anticipated this exact problem and sold you the answer. The skill being practised here is not soldering, it is reading a catalogue.
Stage 4Build the hysteresis yourself ~35 min

Two resistors, and now the gap is yours

The best stage in the project. Put the plain chip back and beat it at its own game.

The CD4093 solved the problem and told you nothing. Worse, it will not tell you how much hysteresis you have — the datasheet promises only 0.3 V and permits 1.6 V, so the chip in your hand is somewhere in a five-to-one range and you are not allowed to know where. If you are designing something that must trip at a known light level, that is not good enough.

So build your own out of ordinary gates. Power off, and put the CD4011 back in.

How it works, before you wire it

Two inverters in a row give you back the signal you started with — not useless, because now you have a strong copy of it. Feed a little of that copy back to the input through a resistor, and the input stops being purely the sensor's business: it becomes a tug-of-war between the sensor pulling through R1 and the output pulling through R2.

Every time the output flips, it yanks the input in the same direction it just went — away from the threshold. That is positive feedback, the same effect that latches the Quiz Buzzer, used here in a smaller dose.

And the dose is a number you control. The gap works out as VDD × R1 / R2. With a 5 V supply and R1 = 10 kΩ: a 1 MΩ feedback resistor gives 0.05 V, 100 kΩ gives 0.5 V, 10 kΩ gives 5 V. You are about to build all three, and one of them is a trap.

Remove the wire from row S to pins 1 and 2 A resistor takes its place
R1 = 10 kΩrow S → a new row, call it X The sensor now pulls on X through a resistor
Row Xpin 1 and pin 2Gate 1, still a NOT
pin 3pin 5 and pin 6Gate 2, the second NOT
R2 = 100 kΩpin 4 → row XThe feedback. This one wire is the whole stage

pins 12, 13− railThe last spare gate
Why the LED moved to a third gate. A CD4011 output can push about 1 mA. If it has to light an LED and hold a gate input at a valid high at the same time, it sags to two-and-a-bit volts and the next gate cannot tell what it means. One output, one job. Gate 3 costs nothing — it was sitting there with its inputs grounded.

Power up and do the slow-hand test. It snaps, exactly like the CD4093 did — except this time you know the number.

Now change the dose, three times

Swap R2 through all three values and do the slow-hand test each time. Predict first, in the worksheet, then find out.

  • 1 MΩ. Sometimes clean, sometimes it flickers. Which is the worst result of the three — a fault that comes and goes is far harder to find than one that is always there.
  • 100 kΩ. Clean every time, and the on and off points are visibly in different places.
  • 10 kΩ. Once the lamp has gone on it never goes off, no matter how much light you give it. Take the sensor into full sunlight and it still will not budge.
The trap is worth understanding, not just avoiding. With R1 = R2 the gap works out as the whole supply, so the voltage needed to change its mind is a voltage the divider physically cannot reach. The circuit has stopped being a switch and become a memory — one bit, remembering that it once got dark. Which is a useful circuit, and the Quiz Buzzer is built out of two of them. Here, it is a bug.
Every real design picks a number for this. Too little hysteresis and the thing chatters; too much and it sticks. The right answer is "comfortably more than my noise, comfortably less than my signal", and the honest way to choose it is to measure your noise. You just did that with your hand and an LED.
Stage 5Something worth switching on ~25 min

The gate decides, the transistor pushes

One transistor, and the dim indicator becomes a real lamp.

Everything so far has been decided correctly and displayed feebly. A CD4000 output is a thinking part, not a pushing part: about 1 mA, which the Gate Bench already warned you about. A white LED wants ten times that, and the buzzer wants more.

A transistor fixes this by not carrying the chip's current at all. The gate's output does nothing but tickle the base through a 10 kΩ resistor — a few tenths of a milliamp, well within budget — and the transistor lets a much larger current flow from the rail, through the lamp, to ground.

Cover the sensor. The white LED comes on properly — bright enough to be a nightlight, ten times what the indicator managed, and the chip is working less hard than before.

  • Leave the little indicator LED connected as well. Watching both confirms the transistor is following the gate and not inventing opinions.
  • Swap the white LED for the active buzzer and you have a drawer alarm: it screams when the drawer opens and the light gets in. Reverse the sensor and the fixed resistor to make it scream when the light goes out instead.
  • Point the white LED at the sensor. If the light it makes is enough to turn itself off, you have built an oscillator by accident — and with the CD4011 fitted and no hysteresis, a fast one. This is the best accident in the box.
That last one is worth doing on purpose. Lamp shines on sensor, sensor says "bright", circuit turns lamp off, sensor says "dark", circuit turns lamp on. The loop's speed is set by how fast the LDR responds — tens of milliseconds, so you get a visible blink rather than a tone. Hysteresis sets how far it has to swing each way, so your R2 controls the blink rate. A relaxation oscillator, built out of a bug.
Sensor, threshold, decision, muscle. That is the shape of every control system there is — a thermostat, a cruise control, an autopilot, a fridge. You have now built one of each part and, more usefully, you have seen the specific way the threshold stage fails when nobody thinks about it.
BonusFour directionsas long as you like

If it is going to live on the shelf

  1. Pull the light sensor out and put the thermistor in Same two holes, no other change. A thermistor is a resistor that listens to heat instead of light, so the identical circuit is now a fire alarm, or a fan controller, or a freezer-door warning. Squeeze it between finger and thumb to test it. The lesson is the one that matters most in this project: the circuit has no idea what it is sensing — it only ever knew about ohms.
  2. Count the dusks Feed your output into the Score Tower's count input. Now the display shows how many times it got dark — which, pointed at a doorway, is a people counter. This is the payoff for Stage 3: feed a chattering signal into a counter and you get eleven instead of one, so the counter proves your hysteresis is real.
  3. Time the darkness A CD4060 is an oscillator and a fourteen-stage counter in one package. Gate its oscillator with your dusk signal and the count tells you how long it was dark, in whatever units you choose the oscillator's resistor to mean.
  4. The Light Ladder One sensor, a chain of four resistors, four Schmitt gates and four LEDs: a bar that climbs as the light drops. Four thresholds instead of one is a flash converter, which is how the fastest real analogue-to-digital chips work. Warning, and it is the interesting part: the resistor values come out at 140 k, 12 k, 30 k and 100 k rather than anything round, because the trip points bunch up near the threshold. Work out why before you build it.
Section 8

When it doesn't work

Sensor projects fail in their own way: usually the circuit is fine and the threshold is in the wrong place.

What you seeAlmost always
The lamp is always on, whatever the lightThe fixed resistor is too big, so the circuit thinks it is always dark. Go down a value. (After Stage 4, also suspect R2 = 10 kΩ — it latches.)
The lamp is always off, whatever the lightThe fixed resistor is too small. Go up a value. Try covering the sensor completely first — "dark" for a 1 kΩ resistor is darker than you think.
It switches, but at the wrong light levelThat is the fixed resistor's whole job. This is tuning, not a fault. The table in Section 2 is your menu.
It flickers at the crossingYou are on the CD4011 with no feedback resistor, which is Stage 2 behaving correctly. Stage 3 or Stage 4 fixes it.
It flickers sometimesNot enough hysteresis for your noise. R2 is too big — go from 1 MΩ to 100 kΩ.
It goes on and never comes offToo much hysteresis: R2 is close to R1. The circuit has become a latch.
The indicator LED is feebleNormal. About 1 mA from a CD4000 output. Stage 5 is the cure.
The white LED stays dark but the indicator worksThe transistor is in backwards, or the LED is. PN2222 flat face toward you: E – B – C.
It blinks steadily and will not stopYour lamp is shining on your sensor. Either turn it away or keep it — see Stage 5.
Everything changes when you touch the boardAn input is floating, or your finger is the sensor. Check the spare gate inputs are at −.
A chip gets warmPower backwards, or an output wired to a rail. Unplug now.
The technique that finds threshold faults fastest. Put the LED back on the divider itself, straight off row S through 1 kΩ, like Stage 1. If that LED responds to light, the sensor half is fine and the problem is in the gates. If it does not, stop looking at the chip.
