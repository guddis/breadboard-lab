Frequency & sound · Project 04

The Siren
Box

A blinking LED and a musical note are the same circuit. The only difference is how fast. Build one oscillator a thousand times faster than the one in Project 03, hang a buzzer on it, and you have an instrument. Then put a second oscillator in charge of the first one and you have a police siren.

Age 10–12, with an adult nearby for the first hour Time 1½–2 hours, in 5 stages Voltage 5 V — nothing dangerous Chips CD40106, CD4066
Section 1

What you're building

This one makes actual noise in your browser. Turn your volume down first.

Four buttons, four notes. A photoresistor you wave your hand over to bend the pitch. And two switches that put a second and third oscillator in charge — one that flips the pitch back and forth to make a siren, one that chops the sound into beeps.

Hold a button to make a sound, like holding a piano key. The frequencies shown are worked out from the same formula as the guide, using the resistors you'll actually plug in — so what you hear here is what the box does.

The whole idea in one line. A resistor and a capacitor set a speed. At 1 beat per second you see a blink; at 1000 beats per second you hear a note. Nothing else changes. And an oscillator's output is just a signal — so it can be wired to control another oscillator.
Section 2

Sound is just blinking, sped up

If you built Project 03, you have already built this circuit once.

Here is the oscillator from the Reaction Machine, unchanged: one Schmitt-trigger inverter, one resistor, one capacitor, output fed back to its own input. It fills the capacitor until the input counts as a 1, flips, empties it until the input counts as a 0, flips back, and never settles.

In Project 03 the resistor was 1 MΩ and you got 12 beats a second, which your eye sees as a blinking light. Make the resistor 10 kΩ — a hundred times smaller — and you get about 1100 beats a second. Your eye can't follow that at all. But a buzzer can, and your ear hears it as a note.

ResistorBeats per secondWhat it is
1 MΩ12A blinking light (Project 03)
100 kΩ123A low buzzing growl — the bottom of hearing
10 kΩ1 133A clear note, roughly two octaves above middle C
5.1 kΩ2 040Piercing. Also the loudest, and there's a reason
2 kΩ4 168A shriek
1 kΩ6 295Horrible. Some adults can barely hear it
Why the 5.1 kΩ note is the loudest. A buzzer is a physical object with a size and a stiffness, so like a bell or a guitar string it has one frequency it likes best. For most small buzzers that's somewhere around 2 kHz. Feed it its favourite frequency and it gives you far more noise for the same electricity. Engineers call it resonance, and it's why a buzzer's datasheet quotes one frequency.

The formula, with the honest correction

The clean version is f ≈ 1.23 ÷ (R × C). For the slow oscillators in this project that's accurate. For the fast one it is not, and the reason is worth knowing.

A CD4000 output isn't a perfect 0 V or 5 V — it behaves as if it has about 1 kΩ of resistance built into it. When your timing resistor was 1 MΩ, an extra 1 kΩ made no difference at all. But when your timing resistor is 2 kΩ, that hidden kilohm is a third of the total, and your note comes out noticeably flat. So:

f ≈ 1.23 ÷ ( (R + 1 kΩ) × C )

Every number in the table above already has that correction in it. In the worksheet you will measure your own notes, compare them with both formulas, and work backwards to the hidden resistance inside your own chip — which is a genuinely respectable piece of reverse-engineering for an afternoon.

Section 3

One oscillator in charge of another

The idea that turns a beeper into a siren.

An oscillator's output is an ordinary signal: high, low, high, low. So it can be wired to anything a signal can control. Wire a slow one to something that changes the fast one, and the fast one starts changing all by itself.

There are two different jobs the slow oscillators do, and they sound completely different:

  1. WAIL — change the pitch Switch a second resistor in and out of the fast oscillator's timing path. Two resistors side by side make a smaller resistance, so the pitch jumps up, then back down, about once a second. That two-tone nee-naw is a European police siren.
  2. CHOP — interrupt the sound Leave the pitch alone and switch the buzzer itself on and off about twelve times a second. Same note, cut into pieces. That's an alarm clock, or a stutter.

Turn both on and you get warbling beeps. Four combinations, four characters:

WAILCHOPWhat you hear
offoff A steady note. An instrument
onoff Police siren — two tones, nee-naw, nee-naw
offon Alarm clock — one note, beep beep beep
onon Ambulance — warbling beeps, the worst of them all

Why the pitch needs a switch, and not just a wire

The obvious idea is to run a wire from the slow oscillator into the fast oscillator's capacitor and let it push the voltage about. It doesn't work, and the reason is neat: the capacitor has to fill and empty every cycle. Pushing the voltage up makes the filling faster and the emptying slower by almost exactly the same amount, so the total time barely changes. Push hard enough to matter and the capacitor can no longer empty far enough to trip the inverter, so the sound stops altogether.

To change the pitch you have to change the resistor. That needs something that can be a wire when you want it and not a wire when you don't — which is exactly what the CD4066 is. It holds four analog switches: put 5 V on a switch's control pin and its two signal pins are connected; put 0 V on it and they aren't.

An analog switch is not a gate. A gate looks at its inputs and drives its output to a fresh 1 or 0. A CD4066 switch doesn't look at anything and drives nothing — it just joins two pins together, and whatever was flowing keeps flowing, in either direction. It's a relay with no moving parts. That's why it can sit inside a timing network where a gate would wreck it.

The 100 Ω resistor in series with the buzzer is not optional. Some passive buzzers are a coil of wire with a resistance of only about 16 Ω. Wired straight across 5 V that's a third of an amp, which will cook the transistor and flatten your batteries. The 100 Ω holds it to a sensible 40 mA. If yours comes out too quiet, try the 10 Ω instead — but if the transistor gets warm, put the 100 Ω back and leave it there.

Power

4.5 V to 5 V. Three AA cells, or the MB102 module set to 5 V. Both chips run from 3 V to 18 V. Nothing here goes near mains electricity.

Section 5

Rules for not breaking things — including your ears

  1. Don't hold the buzzer against your ear This is the one real hazard in the whole series. A small buzzer a centimetre from your ear is genuinely loud enough to be unpleasant, and the 2 kHz note is the loudest one. Keep it at arm's length. Test at arm's length. Especially don't do it to somebody else as a joke.
  2. Unplug the power before you rewire Every single time.
  3. Both chips are 14-pin, + on 14 and − on 7 A relief after Project 03. Notch to the left, pin 1 bottom-left, numbers run anticlockwise.
  4. Never leave an input dangling Every unused inverter input goes to −, and every unused CD4066 control pin goes to − as well. The CD4066's signal pins are different — they aren't inputs, and they can be left empty.
  5. Never wire an output straight to + or − That's a short circuit through the chip.
  6. The electrolytic capacitors have a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, they can pop.
  7. Warm chip means stop Pull the power and check the wiring — and check that 100 Ω is really in series with the buzzer.
Section 6

The two pinouts

Pin numbering and breadboard rows are covered in Project 01 if you need them.

Read the CD4066 carefully. Its pins come in an order nobody would guess: switch A is pins 1 and 2 but its control is pin 13, at the other end of the chip. Switch B is pins 3 and 4 with control on pin 5. Copy the drawing; don't reason about it.
Stage 1One note ~20 min

Make a sound on purpose

Five components and you have an instrument.

Power the rails: supply + to the top red rail, supply − to the top blue rail, then jumper across to the bottom rails. Put the 100 µF across the rails, stripe to −.

Place the CD40106, notch to the left:

Pin 3 gets used in Stage 4 and pin 5 in Stage 5, so leave those two easy to reach. Now the buzzer, which needs a transistor because a logic chip can only push about 2½ mA and a buzzer wants twenty times that:

FromToWhy
CD40106 pin 210 kΩ → Q1 base (middle leg)The note

Buzzer other legQ1 collector (right leg)

Power up. You should get a clear, unpleasant note. Hold the board at arm's length.

  • Swap the 10 kΩ for the 100 kΩ. The note drops to a low growl.
  • Swap it for the 1 kΩ. The note becomes a shriek. Ask an adult whether they can hear it; there's a fair chance they can't hear it as well as you can.
  • Swap it for the 1 MΩ. The note disappears and becomes a slow ticking — you are back to Project 03's blinking speed, and the buzzer is clicking once per beat.
  • Put the 10 kΩ back.
That last one is the point of the whole project. Nothing changed but one resistor. Ticking, growling, a note, a shriek — one circuit, four things, separated only by speed. Frequency isn't a different kind of thing from blinking; it's the same thing counted faster than you can see.
Stage 2Four notes ~20 min

A keyboard with four keys

Same oscillator. You just get to choose the resistor while it's running.

Take out the 10 kΩ timing resistor and build this instead. The 1 MΩ stays in permanently; each button adds its own resistor beside it, between the same two pins.

✓FromToNote you get
☐1 MΩpin 2 → pin 1Silence (a faint tick)
☐pin 2 → button 1 → 10 kΩpin 11 133 Hz
☐pin 2 → button 2 → 5.1 kΩpin 12 040 Hz
☐pin 2 → button 3 → 2 kΩpin 14 168 Hz
☐pin 2 → button 4 → 1 kΩpin 16 295 Hz

Use diagonally opposite legs on each button — a 4-leg tactile button has its legs joined in hidden pairs, and diagonal is the choice that's always right.

Press one button at a time and you get four notes. Press two at once and you get a fifth, higher than either — because two resistors side by side make a smaller resistance, and smaller means faster.

  • All four buttons give clearly different notes.
  • No button pressed gives a faint tick, not silence, and not a note.
  • Pressing two buttons gives a higher note than either alone.
  • Fill in the pitches you actually hear in the worksheet and compare them with both formulas.
Why the 1 MΩ has to stay in. Without it, letting go of every button would leave pin 1 connected to nothing but a capacitor — a floating input, which is against the rules and makes the chip pick up radio noise and squeal. The 1 MΩ is a bleed resistor: too weak to make a note, strong enough to keep the input honest.
Stage 3The theremin ~15 min

Play it with your hands

One component swap, and the best five minutes in the project.

A photoresistor is a resistor whose value depends on light: a few kilohms in bright light, hundreds of kilohms in shadow. It is exactly the part this oscillator wants, because the oscillator's speed is set by a resistance.

FromToWhy
CD40106 pin 21 kΩ → photoresistor legStops the pitch going stupidly high
Photoresistor other legCD40106 pin 1

Leave the 1 MΩ and the four buttons in place — the photoresistor just joins the crowd. Now move your hand slowly over the sensor. Shadow lowers the pitch, light raises it.

  • Aim a phone torch at it: the pitch should jump right up.
  • Cup your hand tightly over it: the pitch should fall to a growl or a tick.
  • Try to play a tune. You will not manage it, and neither can anybody else — this is why real theremins are famously hard.
Why the 1 kΩ is there. In bright sunlight a photoresistor can drop to a few hundred ohms, and this chip can't push enough current through a resistor that small — the oscillator would go weak and unreliable instead of high. The 1 kΩ sits in series as a floor, so the total can never go below it. It's a limit you choose on purpose so the circuit can't be pushed somewhere it doesn't work. Real designs are full of them.
Stage 4The wail ~25 min

A second oscillator takes charge

Two chips, and suddenly it's a siren.

The second oscillator is the same three components as the first, but slow: it uses the 10 µF capacitor instead of the little ceramic, which is a hundred times bigger, so it runs about a thousand times slower.

Leave CD4066 pins 3, 4, 8, 9, 10 and 11 empty. The WAIL jumper is a single wire you move between two holes, and it is how you choose the sound:

Hold button 1 and move the jumper to pin 4. Nee-naw, nee-naw.

  • Two clear alternating pitches, about one swap per second.
  • The higher of the two should be the one where the switch is closed — two resistors in parallel, so less resistance, so faster.
  • Swap the 100 kΩ for the 10 kΩ and the siren gets frantic. Swap it back.
You just built a modulator. One oscillator changing another one's frequency is called frequency modulation, and it is how FM radio carries sound, how the FM synthesisers behind most 1980s pop music worked, and how a great many electronic instruments make interesting noises. Yours does it once a second with a 10 kΩ resistor. A radio station does it 100 million times a second. Same idea.
Stage 5The chop ~20 min

Cut it into pieces

A third oscillator, a second transistor, and four different machines.

Chopping is a different job from wailing: instead of changing the note, we switch the buzzer itself on and off. Two transistors in series do it — the buzzer only sounds when both are turned on, so Q2 becomes a gate on the sound.

Why Q1 sits on top of Q2 and not the other way round. Q1's base is driven by the audio oscillator, which swings the whole way from 0 V to 5 V, so it has plenty of voltage to spare above whatever Q2's collector is sitting at. Stack them the other way and the note would come out weak and distorted. When you stack transistors, the one with the strongest drive goes on top.
BonusThree more ~10 min each

If you haven't been told off yet

A hearing test that actually works

Hold button 4 — about 6 300 Hz — and ask everybody in the house whether they can hear it, then work down to button 1. Hearing for high frequencies fades with age, so there is a good chance you can hear something your parents genuinely cannot. Write down who hears what at each note; that's a real audiogram, roughly.

Swap the timing capacitor for the 22 pF

Five thousand times smaller than the "104". The formula says the pitch should go up five thousand times, which is far above hearing — and it won't, because at that size the stray capacitance of the breadboard itself is bigger than your capacitor. You will get something unpredictable that changes when you move your hand near the board. That is a genuine and famous limit: past a certain speed the wiring becomes part of the circuit.

Put the active buzzer in instead

Swap the passive buzzer for the active one and every sound turns into the same one-note beep, no matter which button you press. An active buzzer has its own oscillator sealed inside it, so it ignores yours completely and only obeys on and off. Now you know the difference between the two, which the packet usually doesn't tell you.
