Arbitration · Project 05

The Quiz
Buzzer

Four buttons, four lamps, one buzzer. The first person to press lights their lamp — and the other three buttons go completely dead until the host resets. The interesting part isn't the lamp. It's that the machine decides who was first in less than a millionth of a second, which is why it never gets it wrong.

Age 10–12, with an adult nearby for the first hour Time about 2 hours, in 5 stages Voltage 5 V — nothing dangerous Chips CD4013 × 2, CD4012
Section 1

What you're building

Play it here first — including the part where you try to cheat.

This is a device you will actually use. Four players, one question, and whoever gets there first is the only one who gets to answer. The host presses RESET and the next question starts.

Try to tie by clicking two players as fast as you can — the page will tell you how close you got, and how much closer you would need to be. Force a dead heat does what your fingers cannot.

The whole idea in one line. Four one-bit memories, and a gate that watches all four at once. The instant any of them remembers a press, that gate tells the other three to stop listening.
Section 2

How it knows who was first

Three chips, and one idea you haven't met yet.

Each player gets a flip-flop — the one-bit memory from Project 03. Pressing the button stores a 1 in it, and it stays stored after you let go. That much you have built before.

What's new is that the four memories have to compete. Exactly one of them may win, and none of them can know what the others are doing. Engineers call this arbitration, and every computer in the world contains circuits that do it — deciding which program gets the processor next, which device gets to use a shared wire, which of two clicks arrived first.

  1. Every flip-flop reports whether it is still empty A CD4013 gives you two outputs: Q, which is 1 when it has stored a press, and Q̅ ("Q-bar"), which is the exact opposite. Q lights the player's lamp. Q̅ goes off to do the thinking.
  2. One gate watches all four at once The four Q̅ wires go into a single 4-input NAND gate. Its output is 0 only while every one of them is 1 — that is, only while all four memories are still empty. The moment anybody buzzes, their Q̅ drops and the NAND's output jumps to 1. Call that signal LOCKED.
  3. Flip it to get permission LOCKED is backwards for what we need, so invert it. The answer is called READY: 1 when nobody has buzzed, 0 the instant somebody has.
  4. READY is what the buttons are actually asking for Here is the trick. A player's button doesn't decide what gets stored — it only decides when to store. What gets stored is READY. Press while READY is 1 and you store a 1: your lamp lights. Press a microsecond later, when READY has already dropped to 0, and you store a 0: nothing happens at all.
Why that last point matters more than it looks. The obvious design is to put a gate in front of each button so a late press can't get through. It works, but it has a hole in it: a player who holds their button down through the host's reset gets buzzed in the instant the reset ends, before anyone else has a chance. Storing READY instead closes that hole, because a flip-flop only stores on the moment a button goes down, not for as long as it's held. Hold it all you like — nothing happens until you let go and press again.
The free inverter, again. We need exactly one NOT gate, and putting a whole chip on the board for it would be silly. The CD4012 holds two 4-input NANDs and we only need one, so tie all four inputs of the spare together and feed it LOCKED. A NAND with its inputs tied is an inverter. Project 02 used the same trick for the same reason.
Section 3

How fast is "first"?

This is the number that makes the whole device work, and you can calculate it from the datasheets.

When somebody presses, the lockout doesn't happen instantly. The signal has to travel: through the flip-flop, through the NAND, through the inverter, and back to the other three flip-flops. Each step takes time. The datasheets say how much, at 5 V:

StepChipTypicalWorst allowed
Button edge → Q̅ dropsCD4013150 ns300 ns
Q̅ → LOCKED risesCD4012125 ns250 ns
LOCKED → READY dropsCD4012125 ns250 ns
Total — the lockout window 400 ns800 ns

A nanosecond is a thousandth of a millionth of a second. So the window is about 0.4 microseconds — and the datasheets promise it is never worse than 0.8.

Now compare that with people. Two players trying their hardest to press at the same instant will still be a few milliseconds apart. One millisecond is a thousand microseconds. So:

the machine decides in 0.0004 ms
two people tie no closer than about 2 ms
the machine is roughly 5 000 times faster than the closest possible tie

That is the whole reason this device is fair. Not because it's clever — because electricity is absurdly faster than fingers. You cannot produce a tie by hand. In Stage 5 you will try, fail, and then cheat with a jumper wire to see what a real tie looks like.

And this is a real problem, not a toy one. Inside a computer, things that are equally fast collide all the time — two processor cores reaching for the same memory in the same clock cycle. When two signals arrive inside a flip-flop's window, it can end up briefly stuck between 0 and 1, a state called metastability, and it settles randomly. Real chips contain dedicated arbiter circuits whose only job is to resolve those collisions cleanly. Yours resolves them by being far faster than its users, which is the cheapest arbiter there is.

Section 5

Rules for not breaking things

Rule 4 has changed since the last four projects. Read it properly.

  1. Unplug the power before you rewire Every single time.
  2. All three chips are 14-pin, + on 14 and − on 7 Notch to the left, pin 1 bottom-left, numbers run anticlockwise. No 16-pin surprises in this one.
  3. Never leave an input dangling Including the SET pins you aren't using, and both ends of every button.
  4. An unused input goes to whichever rail the gate ignores This is new, and it matters in Stage 3. Until now the rule has been "unused inputs go to −", which is right for a gate you aren't using at all. But an unused input on a gate you are using has to go wherever it won't interfere: on an AND or NAND that means +, because those gates only care about inputs that are 0. On an OR or NOR it means −. Put a NAND's spare input to − and the gate is jammed, permanently.
  5. Never wire an output straight to + or − That's a short circuit through the chip. The CD4013's Q and Q̅ are outputs.
  6. The electrolytic capacitor has a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, it can pop.
  7. Touch something metal before handling chips Static kills CMOS.
  8. Warm chip means stop Pull the power and re-check rules 2 and 5.
Section 6

The two pinouts

Pin numbering and breadboard rows are in Project 01 if you need reminding.

The CD4012's pins 6 and 8 are "no connection". They go nowhere inside the chip. Leave them empty — don't ground them out of habit, and don't count along the row assuming every pin does something.

You will use each CD4013 flip-flop like this, and the two halves of the chip are wired identically apart from their pin numbers:

Pin jobGoes toWhy
DREADYWhat gets stored: 1 if nobody has buzzed yet
CLOCKThe player's buttonWhen to store it
RESETThe host's buttonClears it back to 0
SET− railNot used — but it must be held at 0
QThe player's lamp1 means "this player buzzed"
Q̅The CD40121 means "this one is still empty"
Why the lamp goes on Q and the logic on Q̅. An LED drags whatever drives it down to about 2½ volts, which is too vague for another chip to read reliably — that was the hard-won lesson of Project 03. Here you get it for free: the flip-flop has two separate outputs, so the loaded one lights the lamp and the clean one does the thinking. Look for spare complementary outputs; they solve this problem constantly.

Stage 2Two players ~20 min

Build the problem on purpose

The second half of the same chip. Then you'll see why the rest of the project exists.

The CD4013 has a second flip-flop with the same jobs on different pins. Wire player 2 onto it:

Now play a round with somebody. Ask a question. Both of you press.

  • Both lamps light.
  • Neither lamp cares that the other one is on.
  • There is no way to tell who was first. The machine has no opinion.
This is the whole problem, and it's worth sitting with for a minute. Each flip-flop is doing its job perfectly. Nothing is broken. What's missing is that neither of them knows the other exists — and remembering something is not the same as competing for the right to remember it. Everything in Stage 3 is about connecting the two so that the first one to fire shuts the other up.

  • The first person to press lights their lamp.
  • The other player's button now does nothing at all, however hard they mash it.
  • RESET clears both and the next round starts.
  • Now the cheat test: have player 2 hold their button down before you press RESET. Press RESET. Player 2's lamp should stay dark. They have to let go and press again like everybody else.
That last test is the design working. The flip-flop stores on the instant its clock input goes up, not while it's held there. A held button has already gone up — there's no fresh moment for the chip to notice. Engineers call the difference edge-triggered versus level-triggered, and choosing between them is one of the most consequential decisions in digital design. Here it's the difference between a fair game and a game where the sneakiest player always wins.

  • All four lamps work, and exactly one can be on at a time.
  • Any player can win. Try each of them.
  • The blue lamp is dimmer than the others. That's the LED, not a fault.

Four players cost you no new thinking, and that's the point. The lockout gate had four inputs from the start, so going from two players to four was copying, not designing. Good digital design looks like this: get one channel right, then repeat it. It's also why a chip with 4 gates in it costs about the same as a chip with one.

Now it's a real device. White lamp on means the board is armed and the host can ask the question. Somebody buzzes: their colour lights, the buzzer sounds, the white lamp goes out. RESET starts the next round.

Now try to produce a tie

Get somebody to help and count down: three, two, one, press. Do it twenty times.

  • Exactly one lamp lights, every single time.
  • You will never get two. You are trying to hit a 0.4 microsecond window with your hands, and your best is a couple of thousand microseconds.

Then cheat

Take a jumper wire and bridge the P1 and P2 button nodes together, so one press arrives at both flip-flops down the same piece of copper. Press.

  • Both lamps light. Two winners, no answer.
  • Now you have seen a genuine dead heat — and you had to build a wire to get one.
What just happened, and what real machines do about it. Both clock edges arrived inside the lockout window, so both flip-flops sampled READY while it was still 1. The machine wasn't wrong; it was asked an impossible question. Computers hit this constantly and cannot solve it by being faster, because the things colliding are just as fast as they are. They use arbiter circuits — two flip-flops wired to fight each other until one wins — and even those can hesitate for an unpredictable moment first. Your buzzer avoids the whole problem by being five thousand times faster than a human, which is a completely legitimate engineering answer.
BonusThree upgrades ~15 min each

If you're going to use it at Christmas

Bright lamps

Give each player's Q output a 10 kΩ to a PN2222 base, and hang the LED from + through 330 Ω to the collector, exactly like the READY lamp. Four times brighter and visible across a room. You'll need four more transistors than the kit has two spare of, so do it for the two players who complain most.

A host lockout button

Add a fifth button wired so that pressing it pulls all four CLOCK lines low and holds them — or more simply, put a button in the + rail feeding the four player buttons. Now the host can arm the board only when they've finished reading the question, which stops people buzzing early on purpose.

Keep score on the seven-segment display

LOCKED goes up once per round and stays up until RESET, so it makes exactly one clean edge per buzz. Feed it to a CD4026's clock and you have a round counter. Counting each player's score needs one CD4026 each, which is four chips and a lot of wire — worth knowing that the lazy version is a pencil and paper, and that real engineers pick that answer more often than you'd think.
