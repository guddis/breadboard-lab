Logic gates · Project 01

The Gate
Bench

Build one small circuit — two switches and three lights — and then swap six different chips through it. Each chip has a different opinion about your two switches. By the end you can work out which chip somebody plugged in while your back was turned, using nothing but the lights.

Age 11+, with an adult nearby for the first hour Time 1½–2 hours, in 5 stages Voltage 5 V — nothing dangerous Chips six, one at a time
Section 1

What you're building

Play with it here first. The real one behaves exactly like this.

Two jumper wires, A and B. You plug each one into the + rail or the − rail. A yellow lamp next to each shows which you chose. In the middle sits a chip, and on the right a red lamp shows what the chip made of your two choices.

Pick a chip below and start flipping. The table on the right fills in a row every time you try a combination you haven't tried yet — four rows in total, and then you know that chip completely.

Click a jumper to flip it between 1 and 0. Mystery chip hides the label and picks one at random — find out which it is by testing it, then name it. That is the game you'll play for real in Stage 5.

The whole idea in one line. A logic chip is a rule about wires. There are only sixteen possible rules for two inputs, your kit holds chips for six of them, and four experiments pin any chip down completely.
Section 2

One wire, two words

Thirty seconds here saves an hour later.

A wire in this project is only ever doing one of two things. It is connected to the + rail, or connected to the − rail. There is no in-between and no "a bit on".

Connected to +

About 5 volts. Engineers write this as 1 and say "high". You'll also hear true.

Connected to −

Zero volts. Written 0, said "low". Also false. This is not the same as unplugged.

Unplugged is a third thing, and it causes trouble. A chip input with nothing on it does not read 0. It drifts, picks up mains hum and radio from the room, and flickers. Every input on your bench goes to +, or to −, or to another chip's output. Never to nowhere.

A logic gate has one or two input wires and one output wire. It looks at its inputs, applies one fixed rule, and drives its output to 1 or 0. No clock, no program, no memory — change an input and the output follows within a few billionths of a second.

The rule is written as a truth table: every possible combination of inputs down the left, what the output does on the right. Two inputs give four combinations, so four rows. That table is the chip. Learn the table and there is nothing else to know.

Section 3

The six gates on your bench

One chip each. You will fill in all six tables by hand.

Each chip below holds several copies of the same gate. A CD4081 is four separate AND gates sharing one lump of plastic — they don't interact, you get four for the price of one. You'll use one of them and tie the spare inputs down.

Read the symbols, not only the names. The flat-backed bullet shape is AND, the curved shield is OR, the shield with an extra arc is XOR, and the triangle is NOT. A small circle on the output means and then flip it — which is the entire difference between AND and NAND, and between OR and NOR. That circle is the most useful piece of notation in digital electronics.
Section 4

Why one bench does all six

The lazy discovery that makes this project short.

Five of your six chips have identical pinouts. Not similar — identical. The inputs of the first gate are always pins 1 and 2, and its output is always pin 3. The three spare gates are always on pins 5 + 6, 8 + 9 and 12 + 13, with outputs on 4, 10 and 11. Power is always pin 14, ground always pin 7.

So you build the wiring once. To meet a new gate you pull the power, lift one chip out, drop the next one in, and power up again. You move zero wires. Four of your six chips arrive that way, with no rewiring at all.

This is not a coincidence. It's a standard, agreed between manufacturers in the 1970s so that a circuit board could take a different chip without being redesigned. The same instinct is why a USB plug fits every USB socket. Standards are boring right up to the moment they save you an afternoon.

The sixth chip, the CD4069, breaks the pattern — it holds six NOT gates instead of four 2-input gates, so it cannot possibly use the same pins. That one costs you a rewire, and Stage 4 walks through it carefully, because three of the holes you have been grounding are outputs on that chip.

Section 6

Six rules for not breaking things

Read once now. Re-read rule 2 every single time you place a chip — which, in this project, is often.

  1. Unplug the power before you swap a chip or move a wire Every time. You will do this five times today, and a live swap is the one that kills a chip.
  2. The notch tells you which way round the chip goes Every chip has a notch or a dot at one end. Notch to the left, and pin 1 is the bottom-left. Backwards means pin 14 gets ground and pin 7 gets +5 V; the chip heats up and dies in seconds.
  3. Never leave an input dangling Including inputs on the three gates you aren't using. Tie every unused input to −.
  4. Never wire an output to + or − That's a short circuit through the chip's own transistors. It matters here more than usual: the holes you ground for one chip may be outputs on the next one.
  5. The electrolytic capacitor has a right way round The stripe marks the negative leg. Backwards, it can pop.
  6. Touch something metal before you pick up a chip Static off a carpet is thousands of volts, and CMOS hates it. Hold chips by the ends, not the legs.
Warm chip means stop. Pull the power immediately, then check rule 2, then rule 4. A chip that has been hot for a few seconds usually survives; one that has been hot for a minute usually doesn't.
Section 7

Reading a chip, and reading a breadboard

Two skills, ten minutes. After this the wiring is following a list.

Pin numbering

How a breadboard is joined up

Some cheap boards split the rails in the middle. If the left half of your board works and the right half is dead, that is why. Bridge the two halves with a jumper and carry on.

The six pinouts

Five identical, one different. You only have to hold one of these in your head.

  • The red lamp comes on for exactly one of the four combinations.

All three lamps are deliberately dim, and equally dim. A CD4000 output behaves like a 5 V battery with about a kilohm of resistance built in, so it can push roughly 3 milliamps and no more. Rather than let the two input lamps run bright off the rails and make the output look broken by comparison, the 1 kΩ resistors hold them back to match. Matching them is a choice, not a rule — and it makes the truth table readable at a glance.
What you have actually built. A test bench: a fixed setup whose only job is to let you ask the same question of many different parts. Every electronics lab has one, usually the oldest and ugliest thing on the shelf. Building the bench before the experiment is most of what makes an experiment quick.
Stage 2Two more gates, no rewiring ~15 min

Swap the chip, keep the wiring

Two chips, two tables, and not a single wire moved.

Pull the power. Lift the CD4081 out — rock it gently from both ends rather than levering one end, or you'll bend the legs. Drop the CD4071 in, notch to the left, same holes. Power up.

Test all four combinations again and fill in worksheet table 2. Then do the same with the CD4070 for table 3.

ChipGateWhat to notice
CD4071OR Three of the four rows light. The row that stays dark is the interesting one.
CD4070XOR Two rows light. Compare it against OR: only the bottom row differs.

Put the two tables side by side. OR and XOR agree on three rows out of four, and disagree only when both inputs are 1. In everyday speech "or" almost always means XOR — "you can have pudding or ice cream" is not an invitation to have both. The chip called OR is the one that allows both.

A gate you can hear yourself using. XOR is the "different?" gate: its output is 1 exactly when the two inputs disagree. That makes it a one-bit comparator, and it is why you'll use it for a stair light in Stage 5.
Why swapping is the whole trick. You met two new parts in fifteen minutes because the interesting difference between them is not in the wiring, it's in the rule. Isolating the thing that varies — and holding everything else fixed — is the same move a scientist makes with a control group.
Stage 3The gates with a circle ~20 min

Say no to everything

Two more swaps, and then a gate you make rather than buy.

Same routine. Power off, out with the CD4070, in with the CD4011. Fill in worksheet table 4. Then the CD4001 for table 5.

Now put four tables next to each other: AND, NAND, OR, NOR. Every single row of NAND is the opposite of AND. Every row of NOR is the opposite of OR. The little circle on the symbol means precisely that and nothing more — work out the answer, then flip it.

NOR is the "nothing is happening" gate. Its output is 1 only when both inputs are 0. If you want a light that comes on when no alarm anywhere is triggered, that's a NOR — and it is why the gate exists.

Make a NOT gate out of a NAND gate

You have a NAND on the board. A NAND outputs 0 only when all its inputs are 1. So tie both inputs to the same wire, and only two cases remain: both 0, or both 1. The output is then 1, then 0. That is a NOT gate.

Put the CD4011 back in and add one wire:

FromToWhy
A columnpin 2 Both inputs of gate 1 now follow A
B jumperPark the free end in − Out of the way, and not dangling

Flip A on its own. The red lamp does the opposite of the A lamp, every time. Fill in worksheet table 6 — it has only two rows, because a gate with one meaningful input only has two.

You just did what a chip factory does. NAND can imitate every other gate: NOT, AND, OR, all of them. It is functionally complete, and that matters commercially — it is often cheaper to build a processor out of one repeated NAND cell than to manufacture five different kinds of gate. The bonus stage builds AND and OR out of NANDs so you can see it for yourself.
Stage 4The odd chip out ~15 min

The chip that only says no

The one rewire in the project. Read the warning before you touch anything.

The CD4069 holds six NOT gates. Six gates with one input each need twelve pins, arranged input-output-input-output all the way round, so its pins mean completely different things from the five chips you've used so far.

Pull the six ground jumpers out first. On the quad chips, pins 5, 6, 8, 9, 12 and 13 are all inputs and grounding them is correct. On the CD4069, pins 6, 8 and 12 are outputs. Leave those jumpers in, insert this chip, and you have wired three outputs straight to ground — rule 4, and the chip will get hot. Take them out while the power is off, then put the CD4069 in.

FromToWhy

Move the output lampfrom pin 3 to pin 2 Gate 1's output lives on pin 2 here
A columnpin 1Already there — leave it
pins 3, 5, 9, 11, 13− rail The five unused inputs on this chip
B jumperPark the free end in − This chip has nothing for B to do

Power up and flip A. The red lamp is on when the A lamp is off. It is the same behaviour you built out of a NAND in Stage 3, now coming from a chip that does nothing else.

Two NOTs in a row

You have five spare inverters, so chain two of them: send gate 1's output into gate 2's input, and watch gate 2's output.

FromToWhy
pin 2pin 3First NOT feeds the second
Move the output lampfrom pin 2 to pin 4 Watch the second gate instead

The red lamp now copies the A lamp exactly. Two flips cancel out, and you have built a gate that does nothing at all.

Doing nothing is a real job. A pair of inverters is called a buffer, and factories sell chips full of them. The output is a fresh, strong copy of a signal that may have arrived tired — because one chip output can only feed so many inputs before its voltage sags. When a signal has to travel across a large board or fan out to many places, somebody puts buffers in. Not every useful circuit changes its input.
Stage 5Make it do a job ~30 min

Two machines and a guessing game

Same bench. Now the gates are useful instead of interesting.

Machine one: the two-hand interlock

Dangerous machinery in factories often has two start buttons, placed far enough apart that you need both hands. If both your hands are on the buttons, neither is inside the machine. That is an AND gate with a safety certificate.

Power off. Put the CD4081 back in, restore the six ground jumpers on pins 5, 6, 8, 9, 12 and 13, and put the output lamp back on pin 3. Then replace the two loose jumpers with buttons:

FromToWhy
Button 1, one leg+ railPressing connects A to +
Button 1, diagonally opposite legA column Diagonal legs are the pair that is always right
A column10 kΩ → − rail Pull-down: holds A at 0 when the button is up
Button 2, one leg+ rail
Button 2, diagonally opposite legB column

pin 3220 Ω → green LED long legThe GO light
Green LED short leg− rail

Green comes on only while both buttons are held. Let either one go and it dies instantly.

The pull-down resistor is the whole reason this works. A button that isn't pressed doesn't connect its input to 0 — it connects it to nothing, and rule 3 says nothing is trouble. The 10 kΩ resistor to − holds the input at 0 whenever the button is up, and is weak enough that pressing the button overrules it easily. Almost every button on almost every circuit board has one of these.

Machine two: the stair light

A staircase has a switch at the bottom and a switch at the top, and either one turns the light on or off no matter what the other one is doing. Think about what that rule actually says: the light changes state whenever either switch changes. It doesn't care about the switches' positions, only whether they agree.

That is XOR. Power off, swap the CD4081 for the CD4070, put the two loose jumpers back in place of the buttons, and move the lamp back to the red LED — or leave the green one in, it makes no difference to the logic.

Now flip either jumper between + and −. The lamp changes every time, whichever jumper you touch.

This is the real circuit in your house. Two-way switching on a staircase does exactly this, using mechanical switches and no chips — but the logic is identical, and an electrician who wires it wrong gets a light that only works when the upstairs switch is down. Your bench version has an advantage: you can see both inputs and the answer at once.

The guessing game

Put the bench back to its Stage 1 state: two loose jumpers, red output lamp on pin 3, six ground jumpers in place. Now have somebody else pick one of the five quad chips — CD4081, CD4071, CD4070, CD4011 or CD4001 — and plug it in while you look away.

Your job is to name the gate. You may test any combination you like. Count how many tests you need, and write the game down in worksheet table 7.

Three tests are always enough, and sometimes necessary. Each test gives you one bit of information — the lamp is on or off — so three tests can tell apart at most eight possibilities. Two tests can only separate four, and you have five chips. So three is both sufficient and the true minimum. Start with A=0, B=0: that one test splits your five chips into the two that light and the three that don't.
This is how faults get found. Nobody hands an engineer a broken circuit with a label on it. You form a guess, choose the test that would rule out the most possibilities, and repeat. Choosing the test that halves the field — instead of the first test you happen to think of — is the difference between five minutes and an afternoon.

Three things to try next

Pick whichever one you like the sound of.

1. NAND is enough for everything

Put the CD4011 in. You have four NAND gates and you have already turned one of them into a NOT. A NAND followed by a NOT is an AND — so use two gates:

OR is stranger. Invert both inputs first, then NAND the results:

Three gates, and the red lamp now behaves exactly like the CD4071 did in Stage 2.

You've just proved De Morgan's law with wire. "Not A, and not B" being false is the same statement as "A or B" being true. Augustus De Morgan wrote that down in the 1840s, a century before anyone could build it, and now it's the rule that lets a factory make a whole processor from one repeated cell.

2. Make it bright

The dim lamps are the chip's fault, not yours, and one transistor fixes it. A PN2222 with the flat side toward you and the legs down reads E – B – C: emitter, base, collector.

FromToWhy
Gate output pin10 kΩ → base (middle leg) A tiny current, all the chip can afford

+ rail220 Ω → white LED long leg Current straight off the rail now
White LED short legCollector (right leg) The transistor is the switch

About a third of a milliamp goes into the base and about eight milliamps flow through the LED — roughly thirty times what the chip could manage on its own, and bright enough to read by.

Chips think, transistors shove. Keep those two jobs separate in your head and a lot of electronics stops being confusing. If something has to be bright, loud, or move, a logic chip will not do it alone.

3. Build a gate nobody sold you

There are sixteen possible rules for two inputs — you work out why in worksheet question 8 — and you own chips for six of them. Pick one of the other ten and build it. "Output is 1 only when A is 1 and B is 0" is a good first target: it needs one NOT and one AND, and both are on your bench.

Where this goes next. Every gate you met today forgets everything the instant you change an input. Add a chip that remembers — a CD4013 flip-flop — and you can build a counter, a memory, or a lock that stays locked. The Logic Vault is the next project along: the same gates, wired into a combination safe with an alarm.
Section 8

When it doesn't work

It will. This table is the fastest way out.

What you seeAlmost always
Nothing lights at all, everPower isn't reaching pin 14 and pin 7. Check both, on the chip, with the chip in.
A chip gets warmUnplug now. Either it's in backwards (rule 2) or a jumper is grounding an output (rule 4 — the CD4069 trap).
Yellow lamps work, red never doesThe output lamp is on the wrong pin, or in backwards. Long leg toward the resistor and the chip.
Red lamp is on for all four rowsBoth jumpers are landing in the same column, or a jumper is in a column that isn't reaching the pin you think.
Lamps flicker or change on their ownA floating input. Find the input with nothing on it and tie it to −.
The table you measured matches no gateTwo rows are probably swapped. Redo the test, and say the jumper positions out loud as you write each row.
It worked, then stopped after a swapA leg is bent under the chip instead of in its hole. Lift the chip and look along the row of legs at eye level.
Everything is wrong at onceUnplug, walk away for five minutes, come back and re-read one table. This is what real engineers do.
The one technique worth learning. Don't stare at the whole board. Pick one signal — say A — and follow it: jumper, column, pin, lamp. Test each step. Halve the problem, then halve it again. It is the same idea as the guessing game in Stage 5, pointed at your own wiring.
